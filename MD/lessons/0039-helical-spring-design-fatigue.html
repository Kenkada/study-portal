Unit 4 · Lesson 39

Designing helical springs: trial and error, fluctuating load, concentric springs

About 40 minutes · Bhandari §10.9–10.13, 10.19

Your win: you can size spring wire when Sut depends on d (trial and error), and derive and use the spring fatigue design equation (Eq. 10.22) to find the factor of safety under fluctuating load. You can also prove that nested concentric springs need the same spring index and share load in proportion to d², and show that a minimum-weight valve spring has Pmin = Pmax/2.
Warm-up (Lessons 9 and 38)
Spring stress with the Wahl factor, in terms of C and d?
τ = K·8PC/πd², with K = (4C − 1)/(4C − 4) + 0.615/C.
Which factor covers direct shear only?
Ks = 1 + 0.5/C. K = KsKc adds the curvature effect.
On a Soderberg diagram, what are the two axes?
Mean stress on the horizontal axis, stress amplitude on the vertical axis.

1. Why real spring design is trial and error (§10.9)

The tensile strength of spring wire falls as the wire gets thicker (Tables 10.1 and 10.2, ). For Grade 2 patented wire, for example, Sut = 1570 at 3 mm, 1510 at 3.6 mm, 1480 at 4 mm and 1440 at 4.5 mm. So the allowable stress (0.3Sut or 0.5Sut) depends on d, while the induced stress K·8PC/πd² goes as 1/d². You cannot solve for d in one go ().

  1. Assume d, then read Sut from the table and find τd.
  2. Find the induced τ = K·8PC/πd².
  3. If τ > τd, try the next standard d. Stop at the first d with τ ≤ τd.

Under fluctuating load Bhandari then raises d one size (for example 4 → 5 mm in Ex. 10.14) before checking fatigue.

2. Fluctuating load: which factor goes where (§10.10)

A spring is never fully reversed. A compression spring stays in compression and an extension spring stays in tension, so the wire sees pulsating shear stress. Valve springs see millions of cycles (–406).

Mean and amplitude \[ P_m = \tfrac12(P_{max} + P_{min}),\quad P_a = \tfrac12(P_{max} - P_{min}) \] \[ \tau_m = K_s\frac{8P_mD}{\pi d^3}\qquad \tau_a = K\frac{8P_aD}{\pi d^3} \]

Stress concentration from curvature is treated like a fatigue notch effect, so it is applied to the alternating part only. The mean stress gets Ks (direct shear only). Endurance data (Elmendorf, Eqs 10.20–10.21) for a 0 → S′se pulsating test:

WireS′seSsy
Patented and cold-drawn (Gr. 1–4)0.21Sut0.42Sut
Oil-hardened and tempered (SW, VW)0.22Sut0.45Sut

3. Derivation: the spring fatigue equation (Eq. 10.22)

Derive \(\dfrac{\tau_a}{\frac{S_{sy}}{(fs)} - \tau_m} = \dfrac{\frac12S'_{se}}{S_{sy} - \frac12S'_{se}}\).
  1. A pulsating test from 0 to S′se has τm = τa = ½S′se. That is failure point A. Static failure (τa = 0) happens when τm = Ssy, at point B. The straight line AB is the line of failure.
  2. For a factor of safety (fs), mark D at Ssy/(fs) and draw DC parallel to AB. Every point on DC has the same (fs). This is the design line.
  3. The working point X = (τm, τa) lies on DC. Drop perpendiculars XF and AE to the τm axis.
  4. Triangles XFD and AEB are similar, since DC ∥ AB. So \(\dfrac{XF}{FD} = \dfrac{AE}{EB}\), with XF = τa, FD = Ssy/(fs) − τm, AE = ½S′se, EB = Ssy − ½S′se. That is Eq. 10.22.
  5. Solved for (fs), ready to use: \(\boxed{(fs) = \dfrac{S_{sy}}{\tau_m + \tau_a\,\dfrac{S_{sy} - \frac12S'_{se}}{\frac12S'_{se}}}}\).

4. Worked example (Bhandari Ex. 10.14)

Ex. 10.14 (): a 100 mm eccentric cam with 10 mm eccentricity. The follower spring force varies from 100 N to 350 N, τ = 0.3Sut. Design the spring statically, then find (fs) against fluctuating stress.
  1. Fluctuating duty, so choose oil-hardened SW wire, C = 6. K = 1.2525, Ks = 1 + 0.5/6 = 1.0833. Induced τ = 1.2525 × 8(350)(6)/πd² = 6697.9/d².
  2. Trials (Table 10.2): d = 3, τ = 744 > 0.3(1520) = 456 ✗. d = 3.6, τ = 517 > 444 ✗. d = 4, τ = 418.6 < 444 ✓. Raise it one size for fatigue: d = 5 mm, D = 30 mm.
  3. The follower travel is 2e = 20 mm, so k = (350 − 100)/20 = 12.5 N/mm and N = 81 370(5)⁴/[8(30)³(12.5)] = 18.84 → 19, Nt = 21. δmax = 28.24 mm, solid 105 mm, gap 20 × 0.5 = 10 mm, free length 145 mm.
  4. Pm = 225 N and Pa = 125 N. τm = 1.0833 × 8(225)(30)/π(5)³ = 148.96 N/mm². τa = 1.2525 × 8(125)(30)/π(5)³ = 95.68 N/mm².
  5. Sut (5 mm) = 1440, so S′se = 0.22(1440) = 316.8 and Ssy = 0.45(1440) = 648. Then \((fs) = \dfrac{648}{148.96 + 95.68(489.6/158.4)} = \mathbf{1.46}\). That is reasonable for springs, where 1.5 or less is normal.

5. Concentric (nested) springs (§10.11)

Two (or three) compression springs, one inside the other, with opposite hands of helix so the coils cannot lock together if they buckle or misalign. Advantages: more load in a restricted space, a fail-safe drive (one spring can break and the mechanism still works), and less surge. Used for heavy-duty diesel and aircraft engine valves and for railway suspension (–426).

Derive Eqs 10.23 and 10.26: same material, same maximum stress, same free length, same deflection and same solid length.
  1. Equal stress (taking K₁ = K₂ for now): \(\dfrac{P_1D_1}{d_1^3} = \dfrac{P_2D_2}{d_2^3}\) … (a)
  2. Equal deflection: \(\dfrac{P_1D_1^3N_1}{d_1^4} = \dfrac{P_2D_2^3N_2}{d_2^4}\) … (b). Equal solid length with no inactive coils: d₁N₁ = d₂N₂ … (c). Substitute (c) into (b): \(\dfrac{P_1D_1^3}{d_1^5} = \dfrac{P_2D_2^3}{d_2^5}\) … (d)
  3. Divide (d) by (a): \(\dfrac{D_1^2}{d_1^2} = \dfrac{D_2^2}{d_2^2}\Rightarrow \boxed{C_1 = C_2 = C}\). This also makes K₁ = K₂, which confirms the assumption.
  4. Put D = Cd into (a): \(\boxed{\dfrac{P_1}{P_2} = \dfrac{d_1^2}{d_2^2}}\). The load is shared in proportion to the wire cross-section area.
  5. Radial clearance: \(c = \tfrac12[(D_1 - D_2) - (d_1 + d_2)]\). Bhandari takes the diametral clearance 2c = d₁ − d₂. Then D₁ − D₂ = 2d₁, so C(d₁ − d₂) = 2d₁ and \(\boxed{\dfrac{d_1}{d_2} = \dfrac{C}{C - 2}}\).

Nesting springs of different free lengths gives a stepped (non-linear) force–deflection curve. The shorter spring joins in only after the longer one has compressed by the difference. Governors of variable-speed engines use this (Ex. 10.19, in Lesson 47).

6. Surge, minimum-weight design and shot peening

  • Surge (§10.13): if the forcing frequency matches the spring's natural frequency, a wave of coil compression runs up and down the spring. The stresses become huge and valve springs fail by fatigue. Natural frequency in Hz (k in N/m, spring mass m in kg): \(f = \tfrac12\sqrt{k/m}\) between two plates, and \(\tfrac14\sqrt{k/m}\) with one end free. Remedies: make the natural frequency 15–20 times the forcing frequency, add friction dampers on the central coils, or use stranded wire (–433).
  • Shot peening (§10.19): small steel balls hit the wire surface and leave a layer of residual compressive stress. Fatigue cracks grow only under tension, so fatigue life rises. It works for springs loaded in one direction ().
Derive (§10.12): a valve spring must hold Pmin when closed and give a fixed stroke Δ, with τ ≤ τmax when fully open. Show that the lightest spring has Pmin = ½Pmax.
  1. Weight of the active coils: \(W = \rho\,\tfrac{\pi}{4}d^2(\pi DN) = \dfrac{\pi^2\rho}{4C^2}D^3N\).
  2. Full stress at full opening: \(\tau_{max} = K\dfrac{8P_{max}C^3}{\pi D^2}\Rightarrow P_{max} = aD^2\), with \(a = \dfrac{\pi\tau_{max}}{8KC^3}\).
  3. Stroke: \(\Delta = \dfrac{8C^4N}{GD}(P_{max} - P_{min})\Rightarrow N = \dfrac{GD\Delta}{8C^4(aD^2 - P_{min})}\). So for a fixed C, \(W \propto \dfrac{D^4}{aD^2 - P_{min}}\).
  4. \(\dfrac{d}{dD}\Big[\dfrac{D^4}{aD^2 - P_{min}}\Big] = 0\Rightarrow 4(aD^2 - P_{min}) = 2aD^2\Rightarrow aD^2 = 2P_{min}\Rightarrow \boxed{P_{min} = \tfrac12 P_{max}}\) (Eq. d, ).

7. Practice (Bhandari Examples)

Ex. 10.13 (): cam spring, preload 50 N, maximum 150 N, d = 3 mm, D = 18 mm, VW wire Sut = 1430. Factor of safety against fluctuating stress? (Also on your slides, p. 787.)

C = 6, K = 1.2525, Ks = 1.0833, Pm = 100, Pa = 50. τm = 183.91, τa = 106.32. S′se = 314.6, Ssy = 643.5. 106.32/(643.5/fs − 183.91) = 157.3/486.2, so (fs) = 1.26.

Ex. 10.11 (): car clutch, 2 pairs of surfaces, 300 N·m, d = 170, D = 270 mm, μ = 0.35, nine springs, C = 5, Grade 2 patented wire, τ = 0.3Sut. Wire diameter after trial and error? (Use Sut = 1570, 1510, 1480, 1440 for d = 3, 3.6, 4, 4.5.)

Uniform wear: P = 4(150 × 10³)/[0.35(440)] = 3896.1 N, so 432.9 N per spring. τ = 1.3105 × 8(432.9)(5)/πd² = 7223.3/d². d = 3: 802.6 vs 471 ✗. 3.6: 557.4 vs 453 ✗. 4: 451.5 vs 444 ✗. 4.5: 356.7 vs 432 ✓, so d = 4.5 mm. D = 22.5, Nt = 8, δ = 7.09, free length 51 mm.

Ex. 10.12 (): tension spring balance, 25 kg capacity, 100 mm scale in 50 divisions of 0.5 kg each, C = 6, SW wire (d = 2.5 mm works out), τ = 0.5Sut. Number of coils?

Each division is 2 mm for 0.5 kg, so k = 0.5(9.81)/2 = 2.4525 N/mm. D = 15 mm. N = 81 370(2.5)⁴/[8(15)³(2.4525)] = 48, all active (extension spring). Solid length 120 mm.

Ex. 10.15 (): diesel exhaust valve, 32 mm, suction 0.03 N/mm², valve 50 g, lift 10 mm, k = 10 N/mm, C = 8, VW wire. Bhandari takes a 30 N preload, d = 4 mm (Sut = 1400), D = 32. Factor of safety against fluctuating stress?

Minimum preload = 24.13 (suction) + 0.49 (weight) = 24.62 N, so take 30 N. Pmax = 30 + 10(10) = 130 N. Pm = 80 and Pa = 50. K = 1.184, Ks = 1.0625: τm = 108.23, τa = 75.38. S′se = 308, Ssy = 630, so (fs) = 1.85. N = 7.95 → 8, free length 60 mm.

Ex. 10.16 (): compensating valve, preload 100 N, a 40 mm plunger moves 10 mm when p₁ − p₂ = 0.1 N/mm², spring in a 25 mm bore, C = 5, VW wire, d = 3.6 mm (Sut = 1400), D = 18. Factor of safety against fluctuating stress?

Pmax = 100 + (π/4)(40)²(0.1) = 225.66 N. Pm = 162.83 and Pa = 62.83. K = 1.3105, Ks = 1.1: τm = 175.97, τa = 80.89. S′se = 308, Ssy = 630, so (fs) = 1.48. Do = 21.6 mm fits the 25 mm bore. N = 23.3 → 24.

Ex. 10.17 (): concentric valve spring, total 6000 N at 50 mm deflection, τ = 800 N/mm² in each, C = 6, same free and solid lengths, diametral clearance = d₁ − d₂. Force carried by the outer spring?

d₁/d₂ = C/(C − 2) = 1.5, so P₁/P₂ = 2.25. With P₁ + P₂ = 6000: P₁ = 4153.85 N and P₂ = 1846.15 N. Then d₁ = 9.97 → 10, D₁ = 60, d₂ = 6.65 → 7, D₂ = 42. N₁ = 5.67 → 6 (Nt = 8). Equal solid length: 8(10) = (Nt)₂(7), so 11.43 → 12 and N₂ = 10.

Ex. 10.20 (): exhaust valve spring, preload 375 N, τmax = 750 N/mm², Do ≤ 42 mm, minimum weight. Wire diameter?

Minimum weight gives Pmax = 2(375) = 750 N. With d = 42/(C + 1): 750 = K·8(750)C(C + 1)²/π(42)², so CK(C + 1)² = 692.7. That falls between C = 7 (543.4) and C = 8 (767.2); take C = 8, so d = 4.67 → 5 mm and D = 37 mm. Check: C = 7.4, K = 1.2, τ = 678.4 < 750 ✓.

8. GATE corner

In fatigue design of a helical spring, the mean shear stress is multiplied by:

  1. The Wahl factor K, which includes curvature
  2. The shear factor Ks, which excludes curvature
  3. The fatigue notch factor Kf for wire
  4. No factor, since mean stress is steady
Curvature acts like a notch, so it affects only the alternating component. Mean: Ks. Amplitude: K (Eqs 10.18–10.19).

Two concentric springs of the same material, free length and solid length carry a total load. The load on each spring is proportional to its:

  1. mean coil diameter
  2. number of active coils
  3. spring index value
  4. wire cross-section area
P₁/P₂ = d₁²/d₂² (Eq. 10.23), and both springs have the same C.

To avoid surge in an engine valve spring, its natural frequency should be:

  1. equal to the camshaft frequency
  2. half of the camshaft frequency
  3. 15–20 times the camshaft frequency
  4. slightly above the camshaft frequency
Keep well clear of resonance and of its harmonics: fn ≥ 15–20 × forcing frequency (§10.13).

Shot peening improves the fatigue life of springs because it:

  1. leaves residual compressive stress at the surface
  2. raises the tensile strength of the core
  3. removes the curvature stress on inner fibres
  4. increases the modulus of rigidity of wire
Fatigue cracks grow only under tension. A compressive surface layer delays them (§10.19).

Exam traps

  • Read Sut for the final wire diameter when you check fatigue (5 mm in Ex. 10.14, not 4 mm).
  • S′se and Ssy use different multipliers for patented wire (0.21, 0.42) and oil-hardened wire (0.22, 0.45).
  • In Eq. 10.22 the left-hand denominator is Ssy/(fs) − τm, not Ssy − τm.
  • A cam with eccentricity e moves the follower 2e, not e.
  • Concentric springs: the outer spring is the one with the thicker wire, and it carries the larger share.
