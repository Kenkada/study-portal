Unit 2 · Lesson 22

Power screws: torque, efficiency and self-locking

About 30 minutes · Bhandari §6.1–6.13

Your win: for any square or trapezoidal screw you can find the torque to raise and lower a load (with collar friction), the overall efficiency and the effort, and say whether the screw is self-locking.
Warm-up (Lesson 18)
Pitch vs lead?
Pitch: thread-to-thread distance. Lead: axial travel per turn = (number of starts) × pitch.
Why are V threads used for fastening?
High friction resists loosening, the opposite of what a power screw needs.

1. What a power screw is

A power (translation) screw converts rotation into linear motion to transmit power: screw jacks, lathe lead screws, vices, testing machines (). Advantages: large load capacity, compact, simple, large mechanical advantage, precise motion, can be self-locking. Disadvantages: low efficiency (often around 40% or less) and wear.

Multi-start screws move faster and are more efficient (larger helix angle), but give less mechanical advantage and may lose self-locking. Designations: Sq 30 × 6, Tr 40 × 7, Tr 40 × 14 (P7) = two-start ().

2. The screw as an inclined plane

Square threads (Eqs 6.1–6.10) \[ d_c = d - p \qquad d_m = d - 0.5p \qquad \tan\alpha = \frac{l}{\pi d_m} \] \[ M_t = \frac{Wd_m}{2}\tan(\phi+\alpha)\ \text{(raise)} \qquad M_t = \frac{Wd_m}{2}\tan(\phi-\alpha)\ \text{(lower)} \qquad \eta = \frac{\tan\alpha}{\tan(\phi+\alpha)} \]

Maximum efficiency at \(\alpha = 45° - \phi/2\): \(\eta_{max} = \frac{1-\sin\phi}{1+\sin\phi}\). A self-locking square screw has η < 50%.

Trapezoidal / Acme: replace μ by μ sec θ (θ = 15° or 14.5°) (Eqs 6.12–6.16) \[ M_t = \frac{Wd_m}{2}\cdot\frac{\mu\sec\theta + \tan\alpha}{1 - \mu\sec\theta\tan\alpha}\ \text{(raise)} \qquad \eta = \frac{\tan\alpha\,(1-\mu\sec\theta\tan\alpha)}{\mu\sec\theta + \tan\alpha} \]
Collar friction (uniform wear) and overall efficiency (Eqs 6.18–6.20) \[ (M_t)_c = \frac{\mu_c W}{4}(D_o + D_i) = \mu_c W R_m \qquad (M_t)_t = M_t + (M_t)_c \qquad \eta_o = \frac{Wl}{2\pi (M_t)_t} \]

μ ≈ 0.15 for lubricated threads. Collar: Table 6.3 (e.g. soft steel on cast iron 0.12 running). Thrust ball bearing: 0.01–0.02 ().

3. Worked example (Bhandari Ex. 6.1)

Example 6.1 (). A triple-threaded square screw, d = 50 mm, p = 8 mm; collar Do = 100, Di = 65 mm; μ = 0.15 (threads and collar); W = 15 kN; uniform wear. Find the raising torque, the lowering torque, and the force at a 500 mm radius to raise.
  1. l = 3 × 8 = 24 mm; dm = 50 − 4 = 46 mm; tan α = 24/(π·46) ⇒ α = 9.429°; φ = 8.531°.
  2. Thread torque (raise) = 15 000 × 23 × tan(17.96°) = 111 831 N·mm. Collar = 0.15 × 15 000 × 165/4 = 92 812.5 N·mm. Total = 204.6 N·m.
  3. Thread torque (lower) = 15 000 × 23 × tan(−0.898°) = −5408 N·mm (φ < α: the threads alone overhaul). Total = −5408 + 92 812.5 = 87.4 N·m. Collar friction holds the load.
  4. Force at 500 mm = 204 644/500 = 409.3 N.

4. Practice (Bhandari Examples)

Ex. 6.2 (): double-threaded ISO trapezoidal screw, d = 100, p = 12, W = 300 kN, μ = 0.15, no collar friction. Raising torque?

l = 24, dm = 94, tan α = 0.0813; μ sec 15° = 0.1553. Mt = 300 000 × 47 × (0.1553 + 0.0813)/(1 − 0.1553 × 0.0813) = 3378.7 N·m. Lowering 1030.4 N·m; η = 33.9%.

Ex. 6.3 (): machine vice, single-start square Sq 22 × 5; collar 55/45 mm; μ = 0.15 threads, 0.17 collar; operator applies 125 N at 150 mm. Clamping force?

dm = 19.5, α = 4.666°. Thread torque = 2.286W; collar = 0.17 × W × 100/4 = 4.25W. 125 × 150 = 6.536W ⇒ W = 2869 N. Overall η = 2869 × 5/(2π × 18 750) = 12.2%.

Ex. 6.4 (): gate valve, Sq 40 × 7 single start, gate weight 5 kN, frictional resistance 2 kN, thrust washer 40/80 mm, μ = 0.15 threads and 0.12 washer, two arms each at 500 mm. Force per arm to raise the gate?

Raising: W = 5000 + 2000 = 7000 N (friction opposes motion). Thread torque 27 210, collar 0.12 × 7000 × 120/4 = 25 200; total 52 410 N·mm = 2P × 500 ⇒ P = 52.4 N.

Lowering: W = 5000 − 2000 = 3000 N, giving P = 15.6 N. η = 14.9%. Nut: z = 4(7000)/(π × 5 × (40² − 33²)) = 3.49, so 4 threads and a 28 mm nut.

Ex. 6.6 (): lathe lead screw, single-start Tr 52 × 8, axial force 2 kN, collar 100/60 mm, μ = 0.15 threads and 0.12 collar, 30 rpm. Efficiency?

dm = 48, tan α = 0.0531, μ sec θ = 0.1553. Thread torque 10 086; collar 9600; total 19 686 N·mm. Power = 2π(30)(19 686)/60×10⁶ = 0.062 kW. η = 2000 × 8/(2π × 19 686) = 12.9%.

Ex. 6.11 (): differential screw jack, upper screw RH 50 × 12, lower RH 50 × 8, the nut turned by 100 N at 500 mm, μ = 0.15. Load raised?

Upper screw moves up the "incline": M₁ = W(44/2)tan(8.531° + 4.962°) = 5.279W. Lower screw moves down relative to the nut: M₂ = W(46/2)tan(8.531° − 3.169°) = 2.159W.

50 000 = 7.438W ⇒ W = 6722 N. η = 4W/(2π × 50 000) = 8.6%. The load rises (12 − 8) = 4 mm per turn.

Neglecting collar friction, when is a power screw self-locking?

  1. When μ is at least tan α (friction angle ≥ helix angle)
  2. When its efficiency is more than fifty per cent
  3. When it has two or more starts on the thread
  4. When the collar has a thrust ball bearing fitted
Self-locking: φ ≥ α ⇔ μ ≥ l/πdm (Eq. 6.9). Its efficiency is then below 50%. More starts increase α, which works against self-locking.

Why does a trapezoidal thread need more torque than a square thread of the same size?

  1. Its helix angle is always larger
  2. Its mean diameter is always smaller
  3. Its normal force is W sec θ, so friction rises
  4. Its collar friction is always larger
The flank is inclined at θ, so the normal force is W/cos θ and the friction is μW sec θ. Hence "μ sec θ" in place of μ ().

Exam traps

  • Using the pitch instead of the lead in tan α for a multi-start screw.
  • Forgetting the collar torque, or using it in a self-locking check that asks about the threads alone.
  • In a gate valve, the axial load differs for raising (W + F) and lowering (W − F).
