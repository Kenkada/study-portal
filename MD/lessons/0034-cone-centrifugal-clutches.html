Friction clutches II: cone and centrifugal clutches; energy and heat

About 35 minutes · Bhandari §11.5–11.8

Your win: you can derive the torque of a cone clutch (and why α ≈ 12.5°), the torque of a centrifugal clutch from shoe mass and speeds, and the energy dissipated during engagement, which comes out independent of torque. You can then design all three.
Warm-up (Lesson 33)
Uniform wear plate-clutch torque?
Mt = μP(D + d)/4
Where is pressure greatest under uniform wear?
At the inner radius (pr = constant).

1. Cone clutch: derivation

An inner cone (lined with leather, cork or asbestos) slides on splines into an outer cone keyed to the driver. A spring engages it and a fork disengages it. The wedge action gives a large normal force from a small axial force. Drawback: the shafts must be accurately co-axial ().

Derive Eqs 11.11–11.19 for semi-cone angle α.
  1. An elemental frustum between r and r + dr has slant width dr/sin α, so area dA = 2πr dr/sin α.
  2. Normal force p dA; friction μp dA; torque μp dA·r = \(\dfrac{2\pi\mu p r^2 dr}{\sin\alpha}\).
  3. Axial component of the normal force: p dA sin α = 2πp r dr. That is the same as for a flat plate, so P = 2π∫pr dr is unchanged.
  4. So every plate result is simply divided by sin α. Uniform wear: \(\boxed{M_t = \dfrac{\mu P(D+d)}{4\sin\alpha}}\), P = πpad(D − d)/2. Uniform pressure: \(M_t = \dfrac{\mu P}{3\sin\alpha}\dfrac{D^3-d^3}{D^2-d^2}\).
  5. Face width of lining: \(b = \dfrac{D - d}{2\sin\alpha}\).
  6. Choice of α: smaller α means more torque (∝ 1/sin α), but if α < the friction angle φ (tan⁻¹ 0.2 = 11.3°) the cone self-engages (grabs) and is hard to release. So α = 12.5°. At 12.5° a cone carries 1/sin 12.5° = 4.62× the torque of a plate of the same diameters and force ().

2. Centrifugal clutch: derivation

Shoes on a spider (input shaft) are held in by springs. As speed rises, centrifugal force overcomes the springs and the lined shoes press on the drum (output shaft). It engages automatically and smoothly once the engine is running. Applications: mopeds, chain saws, lawnmowers, golf carts (light duty); cranes, cement and ball mills, even tanks (high-inertia loads that need a time delay) ().

Derive the torque of a centrifugal clutch with z shoes of mass m, CG radius rg, drum radius rd (Eqs 11.20–11.23; r in mm, hence the 1000).
  1. Engagement begins at ω₁, where centrifugal force just equals the spring force: \(P_s = \dfrac{m\omega_1^2 r_g}{1000}\).
  2. At running speed ω₂: net radial force on the drum = \(\dfrac{m\omega_2^2 r_g}{1000} - P_s = \dfrac{m r_g(\omega_2^2 - \omega_1^2)}{1000}\).
  3. Friction force per shoe = μ × net force; torque per shoe = friction × rd.
  4. Total: \(\boxed{M_t = \dfrac{\mu\,m\,r_g\,r_d\,z\,(\omega_2^2 - \omega_1^2)}{1000}}\) (N·mm).
  5. Lining size from bearing pressure: net force = pa × l × b, with arc length l = rdθ (θ ≈ 70° per shoe in Bhandari's examples).

3. Derivation: energy dissipated while engaging

Derive the slip time and the heat generated when a clutch (constant torque Mt) connects inertias I₁ (driver, ω₁) and I₂ (driven, ω₂) (Eqs 11.24–11.27).
  1. Equations of motion: I₁α₁ = −Mt (driver slows), I₂α₂ = +Mt (driven speeds up).
  2. Relative speed: \(\omega_{rel}(t) = (\omega_1 - \omega_2) - M_t\left(\dfrac{I_1 + I_2}{I_1I_2}\right)t\).
  3. Slip ends when ωrel = 0: \(\boxed{t_1 = \dfrac{(\omega_1-\omega_2)I_1I_2}{(I_1+I_2)M_t}}\).
  4. Heat = ∫Mt ωrel dt from 0 to t₁. Substituting t₁: \(\boxed{E = \dfrac12\,\dfrac{I_1I_2}{I_1+I_2}(\omega_1-\omega_2)^2}\). It is independent of the clutch torque: a stronger clutch engages faster but dissipates the same energy.
  5. Temperature rise Δt = E/(mc), with c ≈ 500 J/kg°C for steel or cast iron. Cooling follows \(T - T_a = (T_i - T_a)e^{-(Ah/mc)t}\), so frequent engagements build up heat ().

4. Practice (Bhandari Examples)

Ex. 11.10 (): cone clutch, 30 kW at 500 rpm, μ = 0.2, pa = 0.35, α = 12.5°, D = 300 mm, uniform wear. Inner diameter?

Mt = 572 958 = π(0.2)(0.35)d(300² − d²)/(8 sin 12.5°) ⇒ d = 270.8, so 270 mm. b = 30/(2 sin 12.5°) = 69.3 mm. P = 4Mt sin α/(μ(D + d)) = 4351 N.

Ex. 11.11 (): a cone clutch brings a 150 kg rotor (k = 250 mm) from rest to 1440 rpm in 40 s. Heat generated per engagement? (Constant acceleration; work = Mt × angle slipped at the mean speed.)

α = 150.8/40 = 3.77 rad/s²; Mt = Iα = 150(0.25²)(3.77) = 35.34 N·m. Angle = (150.8/2)(40) = 3016 rad. Heat = 35.34 × 3016 = 106.6 kJ. (Dimensions: d = 159.1, D = 177.3, b = 42 mm, P = 455 N.)

Ex. 11.12 (): centrifugal clutch, 20 kW at 750 rpm, 4 shoes, engagement at 500 rpm, rd = 165, rg = 140 mm, μ = 0.3. Mass of each shoe?

ω₂ = 78.54, ω₁ = 52.36 rad/s; Mt = 254 648 N·mm. m = 1000Mt/(0.3 × 140 × 165 × 4 × (78.54² − 52.36²)) = 2.68 kg. Lining (θ = 70°, l = 200 mm): b = 64.3 → 65 mm.

Ex. 11.13 (): 4 shoes of 1.5 kg, rg = 110, rd = 140, μ = 0.3, spring force 700 N at the start of engagement, running at 1440 rpm. Power transmitted?

700 = 1.5ω₁²(110)/1000 ⇒ ω₁ = 65.13 rad/s (622 rpm). Mt = 0.3(1.5)(110)(140)(4)(150.8² − 65.13²)/1000 = 512 785 N·mm, so 77.3 kW.

Ex. 11.14 (): single-plate clutch, 10 kW at 2000 rpm; input 20 kg, k = 75 mm; output (initially at rest) 35 kg, k = 125 mm. Heat generated during engagement?

I₁ = 0.1125, I₂ = 0.5469 kg·m²; ω₁ = 209.44 rad/s. E = ½(209.44²)(0.1125 × 0.5469/0.6594) = 2046 J. Time t₁ = 0.41 s with Mt = 47.75 N·m.

5. GATE corner

Reducing the semi-cone angle of a cone clutch below the friction angle causes:

  1. a sharp drop in its torque capacity
  2. uniform pressure instead of uniform wear
  3. self-engagement and difficulty disengaging
  4. loss of the wedge action between the cones
With α < φ the wedge locks: the cone grabs and needs a large force to release. Hence α ≈ 12.5° > 11.3°.

The energy dissipated as heat during clutch engagement depends on:

  1. the inertias and the initial speed difference only
  2. the clutch torque and the time to engage only
  3. the coefficient of friction and the plate area
  4. the clutch torque and the friction radius
E = ½[I₁I₂/(I₁ + I₂)](Δω)², with no Mt in it. Torque only changes how fast it happens.

In a centrifugal clutch, the torque transmitted is proportional to:

  1. ω₂ − ω₁
  2. ω₂² alone
  3. (ω₂ − ω₁)²
  4. ω₂² − ω₁²
The net radial force = mrg(ω₂² − ω₁²): centrifugal force at running speed minus the spring force set at engagement speed.

Exam traps

  • Cone clutch: dividing P (not Mt) by sin α. The axial force formula is the same as for a plate.
  • Centrifugal clutch: forgetting the spring force (using ω₂² alone), or mixing mm and m.
  • Energy: using I₁ + I₂ instead of I₁I₂/(I₁ + I₂).
