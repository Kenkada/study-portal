Fatigue: fluctuating stress, the S–N curve and the endurance limit

About 25 minutes · Bhandari §5.4–5.9

Your win: you can turn Sut and a description of a part (surface, size, reliability, type of load) into its corrected endurance limit Se. That's the first step of every fatigue problem in the question bank (Q14–Q22).
Warm-up (Lessons 2 and 4)
Recommended (fs) for a ductile part under fluctuating load, and on what?
1.3–1.5, on the corrected endurance limit.
Shear yield strength by MSST and by DET?
0.5 Syt and 0.577 Syt. The same ratios turn Se into Sse.

1. Three kinds of cyclic stress

Mean stress and stress amplitude (Eqs 5.9, 5.10). Tension +, compression −. \[ \sigma_m = \tfrac12(\sigma_{max}+\sigma_{min}) \qquad \sigma_a = \tfrac12(\sigma_{max}-\sigma_{min}) \]

Fatigue failure is time-delayed fracture under cyclic loading, at stresses below Sut and often below Syt. About 80% of mechanical failures are fatigue (). A crack starts at a stress raiser (oil hole, keyway, thread, scratch, inclusion), grows slowly (smooth, fine-fibrous zone), then the remaining section breaks suddenly (coarse, granular zone). There is no warning, unlike the visible yielding of static failure.

2. Endurance limit and the S–N curve

The endurance limit is the maximum amplitude of completely reversed stress a standard specimen survives for an unlimited number of cycles. 10⁶ cycles is taken as "unlimited" (). It is measured on R R Moore's rotating-beam machine: a polished 7.5 mm specimen under constant bending moment sees fully reversed stress once per revolution.

The S–N (Wöhler) curve plots stress amplitude against cycles to failure on log–log axes. Steels flatten out at 10⁶ cycles. Aluminium alloys keep sloping, so they have no true endurance limit. Below 10³ cycles is low-cycle fatigue (design on static strength); above is high-cycle fatigue (design on endurance limit) ().

Axial loading stresses the whole cross-section, not just the surface, so a flaw is more likely to be in the high-stress zone ().

4. Worked example (Bhandari Ex. 5.4)

Example 5.4 (). A linkage rod of steel 40Cr1 (Sut = 550 N/mm²) carries a completely reversed axial load of ±100 kN. Machined on a lathe, reliability 95%, no stress concentration, (fs) = 2, infinite life. Find d.
  1. \(S'_e = 0.5(550) = 275\) N/mm².
  2. Machined, Sut 550 → Ka = 0.78. Assume 7.5 < d ≤ 50 → Kb = 0.85. 95% → Kc = 0.868. No notch → Kd = 1.
  3. \(S_e = 0.78(0.85)(0.868)(275) = 158.26\) N/mm².
  4. Axial: \((S_e)_a = 0.8(158.26) = 126.6\) N/mm². Permissible amplitude \(= 126.6/2 = 63.3\) N/mm².
  5. \(\frac{\pi}{4}d^2(63.3) = 100\,000 \Rightarrow d = 44.8\) mm. That lies between 7.5 and 50 mm, so the assumed Kb is confirmed. Choose 45 mm.

Notice the last step: you assume a size range for Kb, then check it. If the check fails, redo with the other Kb.

5. Practice

Question bank Q15 (): a forged steel bar, 50 mm diameter, steel 40C8 (Sut = 600 N/mm²), reliability 90%. Ka = 0.44, Kb = 0.85, Kc = 0.897, no stress concentration. Find the corrected endurance limit in reversed bending.

\(S_e = 0.44(0.85)(0.897)(0.5\times600) = 100.6\) N/mm². Forging leaves a rough, decarburised skin. Ka = 0.44 more than halves the endurance limit.

Question bank Q19: a cold-drawn steel shaft (Sut = 500 N/mm², Ka = 0.79), d between 7.5 and 50 mm, reliability 90%, no notch, fluctuating torque. Using the distortion energy theory, find Sse.

\(S_e = 0.79(0.85)(0.897)(250) = 150.58\); \(S_{se} = 0.577(150.58) = 86.9\) N/mm² (Bhandari Ex. 5.13).

A bolt's stress varies between 10 N/mm² compressive and 50 N/mm² tensile. What is the stress amplitude σa?

Compression is negative: σmax = +50, σmin = −10.

\(\sigma_a = \frac12(50-(-10)) = 30\) N/mm² and \(\sigma_m = \frac12(50+(-10)) = 20\) N/mm². If you got 20 for σa, you dropped the minus sign on the compressive stress.

Why is the endurance limit lower for axial loading than for rotating bending?

  1. Axial loads always include some mean stress
  2. The whole section is highly stressed in axial load
  3. Axial specimens are rougher than bending specimens
  4. Axial loading causes buckling before fatigue occurs
In bending only the outer skin is near the maximum stress, but axial load stresses the entire cross-section. A larger highly stressed volume is more likely to contain a flaw, so (Se)a = 0.8 Se.

A grey cast-iron part has a machined surface. What surface finish factor does Bhandari use?

  1. 0.78, read from Fig. 5.24
  2. 0.44, as for a forged part
  3. 0.67, as for a hot-rolled part
  4. 1.00, whatever the finish
Graphite flakes already act as internal notches, so machining scratches add nothing: Ka = 1 for grey cast iron ().

Raising the required reliability from 90% to 99% changes Se by what factor?

  1. 0.814 / 0.897, about 0.91
  2. 0.897 / 0.814, about 1.10
  3. 0.99 / 0.90, about 1.10
  4. 0.868 / 0.897, about 0.97
Kc: 90% = 0.897, 99% = 0.814. Higher reliability means a lower allowable endurance limit, so Se drops by about 9%.

Exam traps

  • Forgetting the 0.8 for axial load, or the 0.5/0.577 for torsion.
  • Applying Kb = 0.85 to axial loading. Bhandari's Kb table is for bending and torsion (axial Kb = 1), though some questions state Kb anyway. Use what's given.
  • Assuming Kb without checking the final diameter against the assumed range.
