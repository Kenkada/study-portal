Unit 4 · Lesson 40

Open-coiled helical springs, and helical torsion springs

About 35 minutes · Bhandari §10.2, 10.14 · derivations by Castigliano's theorem

Your win: you can resolve the load on an open-coiled spring into torsion and bending of the wire, and find the shear, bending and principal stresses. You can derive its axial deflection and the rotation of its free end by Castigliano's theorem, and show both reduce to the closely coiled results as α → 0. You can also design a helical torsion spring, whose wire is in bending.
Warm-up (Lesson 38 and SOM)
Closely coiled deflection, in D and in R = D/2?
δ = 8PD³N/Gd⁴ = 64PR³N/Gd⁴.
Strain energy of a bar of length L under torque T, and under bending moment M?
U = T²L/2GJ and U = M²L/2EI.
Castigliano's theorem?
The deflection at a load, in its direction, is ∂U/∂(that load). A rotation is ∂U/∂(couple), using a fictitious couple set to zero at the end.

1. When a spring is "open-coiled"

If the helix angle α is more than about 10°, the coils have large gaps and the plane of each coil is noticeably tilted (). Bhandari only defines this case and restricts his analysis to closely coiled springs. Open coils are rarer, but they appear in exam theory questions, so we derive their behaviour here. The results come from the CodeCogs springs article linked under this lesson's sources. The derivations below re-check them by energy.

2. Derivation: stresses in the wire (axial load W)

Derive the shear, bending, principal and maximum shear stresses in the wire (direct shear and curvature neglected).
  1. Twisting moment \(T = WR\cos\alpha\) and bending moment \(M = WR\sin\alpha\) (figure).
  2. \(\tau = \dfrac{16T}{\pi d^3} = \dfrac{16WR\cos\alpha}{\pi d^3}\) and \(\sigma_b = \dfrac{32M}{\pi d^3} = \dfrac{32WR\sin\alpha}{\pi d^3}\).
  3. Combine them at the surface (Lesson 2): \(\tau_{max} = \sqrt{(\sigma_b/2)^2 + \tau^2} = \dfrac{16WR}{\pi d^3}\sqrt{\sin^2\alpha + \cos^2\alpha} = \boxed{\dfrac{16WR}{\pi d^3}}\). It does not depend on α, so it equals the closely coiled value.
  4. \(\sigma_1 = \dfrac{\sigma_b}{2} + \tau_{max} = \boxed{\dfrac{16WR}{\pi d^3}(1 + \sin\alpha)}\). The normal stress does grow with α. That matters for brittle-type failure (maximum principal stress theory) and for fatigue.

3. Derivation: axial deflection by Castigliano

Derive \(\delta = \dfrac{8WD^3n}{d^4\cos\alpha}\Big(\dfrac{\cos^2\alpha}{G} + \dfrac{2\sin^2\alpha}{E}\Big)\) for n turns.
  1. Wire length: each turn advances 2πR horizontally along a helix tilted at α, so \(L = \dfrac{2\pi Rn}{\cos\alpha}\) (it is 2πRn only when α = 0).
  2. Strain energy (torsion + bending, constant along the wire): \(U = \Big(\dfrac{T^2}{2GJ} + \dfrac{M^2}{2EI}\Big)L = \dfrac{W^2R^2L}{2}\Big(\dfrac{\cos^2\alpha}{GJ} + \dfrac{\sin^2\alpha}{EI}\Big)\).
  3. Castigliano: \(\delta = \dfrac{\partial U}{\partial W} = WR^2L\Big(\dfrac{\cos^2\alpha}{GJ} + \dfrac{\sin^2\alpha}{EI}\Big)\).
  4. With J = πd⁴/32, I = πd⁴/64 and L from step 1: \(\delta = \dfrac{64WR^3n}{d^4\cos\alpha}\Big(\dfrac{\cos^2\alpha}{G} + \dfrac{2\sin^2\alpha}{E}\Big)\). Using 64R³ = 8D³ gives the boxed form above.
  5. Check: at α = 0, \(\delta = 8WD^3n/Gd^4\), Bhandari's Eq. 10.8 ✓. Stiffness is k = W/δ as before.

4. Derivation: rotation of the free end

Derive the angle φ through which the loaded end turns about the spring axis under axial load W.
  1. Apply a fictitious couple M₀ about the spring axis at the free end. Its vector is vertical, along the axis. Resolved along the wire it adds M₀ sin α to the torque. Perpendicular to the wire it adds −M₀ cos α to the bending moment: \(T = WR\cos\alpha + M_0\sin\alpha\), \(M = WR\sin\alpha - M_0\cos\alpha\).
  2. \(\phi = \dfrac{\partial U}{\partial M_0}\Big|_{M_0=0} = L\Big(\dfrac{T}{GJ}\sin\alpha - \dfrac{M}{EI}\cos\alpha\Big) = WRL\sin\alpha\cos\alpha\Big(\dfrac{1}{GJ} - \dfrac{1}{EI}\Big)\).
  3. Substitute L, J and I: \(\boxed{\phi = \dfrac{64WR^2n\sin\alpha}{d^4}\Big(\dfrac{1}{G} - \dfrac{2}{E}\Big) = \dfrac{16WD^2n\sin\alpha}{d^4}\Big(\dfrac{1}{G} - \dfrac{2}{E}\Big)}\) radians.
  4. Since E = 2G(1 + ν), the bracket is \(\dfrac{\nu}{G(1 + \nu)}\). It is positive for every real material, so an open coil always turns as it stretches. It would vanish only for ν = 0 (E = 2G). A closely coiled spring (α = 0) does not turn.

5. How big is the difference? (built on Bhandari Ex. 10.1's spring)

Open- vs closely coiled spring under axial load

With Ex. 10.1's spring (W = 1250 N, D = 42, d = 7, n = 8) wound open at α = 20°, the deflection rises only from 30.34 to 31.48 mm (+3.8%). The end turns about 6°, and σ₁ rises from 390 to 523 N/mm² (+34%), while τmax stays at 390 N/mm². The lesson: the closely coiled formulas are good for deflection up to about 10°. What open coiling really changes is the normal stress and the end rotation. (These values leave out the Wahl factor, as the classical open-coil theory does.)

6. Helical torsion springs (§10.14)

The coils look like a compression spring, but the ends are formed so the spring is loaded by a moment about its axis: Mb = Pr. Uses include door hinges, brush holders, automobile starters, door locks and window shades. The name misleads: the wire is in bending, each section acting as part of a curved beam, not in torsion (–434).

Derive the stress and the angular deflection (Eqs 10.27–10.31).
  1. Curved-beam bending: \(\sigma_b = K\dfrac{M_by}{I} = K\dfrac{32M_b}{\pi d^3}\), with Wahl's factors \(K_i = \dfrac{4C^2 - C - 1}{4C(C - 1)}\) at the inner fibre (the larger one, used for design) and \(K_o = \dfrac{4C^2 + C - 1}{4C(C + 1)}\) at the outer fibre.
  2. Energy over the wire length πDN: \(U = \dfrac{M_b^2(\pi DN)}{2EI}\). Castigliano with respect to Mb: \(\theta = \dfrac{\partial U}{\partial M_b} = \dfrac{M_b\pi DN}{EI} = \boxed{\dfrac{64M_bDN}{Ed^4}}\) radians.
  3. Stiffness (moment per radian): \(\boxed{k = \dfrac{M_b}{\theta} = \dfrac{Ed^4}{64DN}}\) N·mm/rad. Note it uses E, not G.
  4. Practice points: load it in the winding direction, so the working stresses oppose the residual stresses from cold winding. Keep C between 5 and 15 (below 5 the coiling arbor breaks, above 15 pitch control is lost).

7. Practice

Ex. 10.21 (): window-shade torsion spring, Grade 4 patented wire, Syt = 0.6Sut, (fs) = 2, D = 18 mm, Mb = 250 N·mm, E = 207 000, k = 3 N·mm/rad. For the trial d = 1.6 mm (Sut = 2250), what is the induced bending stress?

C = 18/1.6 = 11.25, Ki = [4(11.25)² − 11.25 − 1]/[4(11.25)(10.25)] = 1.071. σb = 1.071 × 32(250)/π(1.6)³ = 665.8 N/mm², below the allowable 0.6(2250)/2 = 675 ✓. The first trial, d = 1.4 (Sut = 2290), gave 985 against an allowable of 687 ✗.

Ex. 10.21 continued: number of active coils for k = 3 N·mm/rad?

N = Ed⁴/(64Dk) = 207 000(1.6)⁴/[64(18)(3)] = 392.5, so 393 coils. A very soft rate needs a lot of wire.

Application on Ex. 10.1's spring (): W = 1250 N, D = 42, d = 7, n = 8, G = 81 370, E = 207 000. If it were open-coiled at α = 20°, what would its axial deflection be?

δ = [8(1250)(42)³(8)/(7⁴ cos 20°)][cos²20°/81 370 + 2 sin²20°/207 000] = 2.627 × 10⁶ × (1.0852 × 10⁻⁵ + 1.130 × 10⁻⁶) = 31.48 mm, against 30.34 mm closely coiled.

Same spring at α = 20°: maximum principal stress in the wire?

16WR/πd³ = 8WD/πd³ = 8(1250)(42)/π(343) = 389.8. σ₁ = 389.8(1 + sin 20°) = 523.1 N/mm². τmax stays at 389.8.

8. GATE corner

Under an axial load, the wire of an open-coiled helical spring is subjected to:

  1. pure torsion only, like a closed coil
  2. pure bending only, like a torsion spring
  3. combined torsion and bending of the wire
  4. direct tension along the wire axis only
The couple WR resolves into T = WR cos α along the wire and M = WR sin α across it.

For the same W, R and d, the maximum shear stress in an open-coiled spring (helix angle α) compared with a closely coiled one is:

  1. the same, 16WR/πd³
  2. larger by (1 + sin α)
  3. smaller by the factor cos α
  4. larger by the factor sec α
τmax = √((σb/2)² + τ²) = (16WR/πd³)√(sin²α + cos²α). It is the principal normal stress that grows by (1 + sin α).

The wire of a helical torsion spring is mainly subjected to:

  1. torsional shear stress
  2. bending stress
  3. direct shear stress
  4. axial tensile stress
The applied moment acts about the coil axis, and each wire section carries it as a bending moment (§10.14).

The free end of an open-coiled spring under axial load would not rotate if the material had:

  1. E equal to G
  2. E equal to 3G
  3. a helix angle of 45°
  4. E equal to 2G
φ ∝ (1/G − 2/E), which is zero when E = 2G, that is ν = 0. Real materials have ν > 0, so the end turns.

Exam traps

  • Open coil: the wire length is 2πRn/cos α. Leaving out sec α loses the cos α in the denominator.
  • Do not mix the R form (64WR³n) with the D form (8WD³n) in one line.
  • Torsion spring stiffness is per radian. Convert a rotation given in degrees or turns first.
  • Torsion springs: use E in the deflection and Ki for the maximum stress.
