Brakes I: energy equations and block brakes

About 35 minutes · Bhandari §12.1–12.5

Your win: you can find the energy a brake must absorb and its torque, analyse a block brake for both directions of rotation, and state when it is self-energising or self-locking. You can also derive the long-shoe equivalent friction coefficient and the pivot position that makes a pivoted shoe free of friction moment.
Warm-up (Lesson 34)
Clutch vs brake: final state?
Clutch: both members rotate together. Brake: both members at rest.
Heat in clutch engagement depends on torque?
No: E = ½[I₁I₂/(I₁ + I₂)]Δω².

1. Brakes and the energy equation

A brake absorbs the energy of a moving system by friction, either to slow it, stop it, or hold it at rest. The energy becomes heat, so heat dissipation is the central problem (). Types: mechanical (block, shoe, band, disc), hydraulic or pneumatic, electrical (eddy-current, hysteresis). Shoe brakes are radial, disc brakes axial.

Energy to absorb (Eqs 12.1–12.5) \[ E = \tfrac12 m(v_1^2 - v_2^2) + \tfrac12 I(\omega_1^2 - \omega_2^2) + mgh \qquad E = M_t\,\theta \]

With uniform deceleration, the drum turns θ = (ω₁ + ω₂)/2 × t (or θ = distance/drum radius for a hoist).

2. Short-shoe block brake (contact < 45°): derivation

Derive the actuating force and the self-locking condition (Eqs 12.6–12.10).
  1. On the drum: braking torque Mt = μNR; block size N = p·l·w (w between D/4 and D/2).
  2. Moments on the lever about O (clockwise drum, friction helping): P·b − N·a + μN·c = 0, so \(\boxed{P = \dfrac{N(a - \mu c)}{b}}\).
  3. a > μc: friction helps (partly self-energising) and P is still positive. This is the desired case.
  4. a = μc: P = 0, a self-locking brake that applies itself. a < μc: P < 0, it grabs uncontrollably. So design for a > μc.
  5. Anticlockwise drum: the friction moment reverses, so \(P = \dfrac{N(a + \mu c)}{b}\) and more effort is needed. Self-energising action depends on the pivot position, μ and the direction of rotation.
  6. A single block bends the drum shaft (unbalanced N). The remedy is two opposed blocks, the double-block brake (railways, cranes).

3. Long shoe: derivation of the equivalent coefficient of friction

Derive μ′ = 4μ sin θ/(2θ + sin 2θ) for a shoe of semi-angle θ (§12.4).
  1. The rigid shoe moves along its line of action to take up wear, so the vertical wear δy is the same everywhere: radial wear δr = δy cos φ, so δr ∝ cos φ.
  2. Radial wear ∝ friction work ∝ p (rubbing speed is the same for all points), so p = pmax cos φ.
  3. Normal resultant: N = ∫ dN cos φ = R w pmax ∫cos²φ dφ over −θ to θ = ½ R w pmax(2θ + sin 2θ). (The sin φ components cancel.)
  4. Torque: Mt = μR²w pmax ∫cos φ dφ = μR²w pmax(2 sin θ).
  5. So Mt = \(\mu N R\cdot\dfrac{4\sin\theta}{2\theta + \sin 2\theta}\) = μ′NR with \(\boxed{\mu' = \dfrac{4\mu\sin\theta}{2\theta + \sin 2\theta}}\). Use the short-shoe formulas with μ′ in place of μ. For 2θ = 120°, μ′ = 1.17μ.
  6. Pivoted shoe: choose the pivot distance h so the friction moment about the pivot vanishes: ∫μ dN (h cos φ − R) = 0 gives \(\boxed{h = \dfrac{4R\sin\theta}{2\theta + \sin 2\theta}}\). Then Mt = 2μR²w pmax sin θ, Rx = ½Rwpmax(2θ + sin 2θ), Ry = μRx. Used in hoists and cranes, though hard to build because the pivot must be close to the drum ().

4. Practice (Bhandari Examples)

Ex. 12.1 (): a solid cast-iron flywheel disc (Ø1 m, 0.2 m thick, ρ = 7200) at 350 rpm is stopped in 1.5 s. Brake torque?

m = 1131 kg; k² = D²/8; ω = 36.65 rad/s; E = ½(1131)(1/8)(36.65²) = 94 950 J. θ = (36.65/2)(1.5) = 27.49 rad. Mt = 3454 N·m.

Ex. 12.2 (): a 1000 kg car at 100 km/h; each wheel I = 0.5 kg·m², rolling radius 0.35 m; engine and transmission I = 2.5 kg·m² at 5× wheel speed; deceleration 0.5g; four brakes. Torque per brake?

KE: car 385 802 + wheels 6299 + engine 196 838 = 588 940 J, so 147 235 J per brake. t = 27.78/4.905 = 5.66 s; θ = (79.37/2)(5.66) = 224.6 rad. Mt = 147 235/224.6 = 655.5 N·m. (Bhandari prints 665.54, an arithmetic slip.)

Ex. 12.3 (): 2500 kg lowered at 1.5 m/s from a drum (R = 0.75 m; drum mass 50 kg, k = 0.7 m) is stopped in 0.5 m. Brake torque?

E = ½(2500)(1.5²) + ½(50)(0.49)(2²) + 2500(9.81)(0.5) = 2812.5 + 49 + 12 262.5 = 15 124 J. θ = 0.5/0.75 = 0.667 rad. Mt = 22 686 N·m.

Ex. 12.4 (): single block brake, 250 N·m, drum R = 200 mm, μ = 0.35; normal force arm a = 200, friction arm c = 50, effort arm b = 500 mm. Actuating force for clockwise rotation?

N = 250 000/(0.35 × 200) = 3571.4 N. P = N(200 − 0.35 × 50)/500 = 1303.6 N (anticlockwise: 1553.6 N). Hinge reaction 2589.5 N. Not self-locking (a/c = 4 > μ). Block: 3571 = 1 × 2w × w, so w = 42.3 → 45 mm, l = 90 mm.

Ex. 12.5 (): double block brake (Fig. 12.6), P = 500 N, μ = 0.35, R = 250 mm. The linkage gives a 1750 N horizontal pull on each arm, and moment balance gives NR = 3478.3 and NL = 4117.6 N. Braking torque?

Mt = μ(NR + NL)R = 0.35(7595.9)(0.25) = 664.6 N·m. The two shoes carry different normal forces: one is self-energising, the other not.

Ex. 12.7 (): pivoted double-block brake, 2θ = 100°, D = 500, w = 100, μ = 0.2, pmax = 0.5. Pivot distance h for zero friction moment?

h = 4(250) sin 50°/(1.745 + sin 100°) = 766.0/2.730 = 280.6 mm. Torque per shoe: 2(0.2)(250²)(100)(0.5) sin 50° = 957 556 N·mm. Rx = 17 063 N, Ry = 3413 N.

5. GATE corner

A block brake is self-locking when (a = arm of normal force, c = arm of friction force about the lever pivot):

  1. a is greater than μc
  2. a is less than or equal to μc
  3. c is less than or equal to μa
  4. a equals c for any value of μ
P = N(a − μc)/b ≤ 0 when a ≤ μc: no effort is needed, so the brake applies itself.

For a long shoe, why is the equivalent coefficient μ′ greater than μ?

  1. The pressure is uniform over the long shoe
  2. The lining material behaves differently when long
  3. Off-centre normal forces add friction but partly cancel in N
  4. The drum radius is effectively increased
The normal forces at ±φ add fully to the friction torque, but only their cos φ components add to the net normal force N. So friction torque/N exceeds μR.

The main advantage of pivoting the shoe at h = 4R sin θ/(2θ + sin 2θ) is:

  1. no friction moment tends to unseat the shoe
  2. the brake becomes fully self-locking
  3. the pressure on the lining becomes uniform
  4. the braking torque doubles for the same force
The moment of the friction forces about the pivot is zero, so the shoe isn't twisted off its seat ().

Exam traps

  • Writing the moment equation with the friction force on the wrong side: draw its direction for the given rotation first.
  • Forgetting the engine and transmission inertia (at its own speed) in vehicle braking energy.
  • Using μ instead of μ′ for long shoes (contact > 45°).
