Unit 1 · Lesson 5

Material selection: iso-strength and iso-rigidity analysis

About 25 minutes · Bhandari §2.20–2.21 · course slides pp. 86–101 (Ashby)

Your win: given two candidate materials, you can work out which gives the lighter (or cheaper) part when both are designed for the same strength (iso-strength) or the same stiffness (iso-rigidity), for a tie, a beam or a shaft.
Warm-up (Lesson 4)
Which property measures stiffness?
Modulus of elasticity E (and G for torsion).
When does stiffness, not strength, govern a design?
When accuracy or stability depends on deflection: machine-tool spindles, gear shafts, long shafts in torsion.

1. The factors (Bhandari) and the method (Ashby)

Bhandari lists the factors to weigh: availability, cost, mechanical properties, manufacturing considerations (). The criterion property depends on the job: endurance limit for a connecting rod, surface hardness for piston rings, low friction for bearings, high friction for brake linings, plasticity for car body panels.

Your professor's slides add Ashby's four steps (slides pp. 91–92):

  1. Translation: write the function, the objective (minimum mass or cost), the constraints (must carry load F without failing, or without deflecting more than δ) and the free variable (usually the cross-section size).
  2. Screening: go/no-go limits (temperature, corrosion, process) remove unsuitable materials.
  3. Ranking: sort the survivors by a material index.
  4. Supporting information: check the top few in detail with supplier data and prototypes.

2. Iso-strength and iso-rigidity, derived once

Iso-strength means every candidate is sized to carry the same load at the same factor of safety. Iso-rigidity means every candidate is sized to have the same deflection. Length is fixed, and the cross-section is the free variable. Eliminate it and the mass depends only on a group of material properties: the material index (NPTEL: Ashby method).

Derivation: a tie of length L carrying axial force F.
  1. Iso-strength. \(\dfrac{F}{A} = \dfrac{S}{(fs)}\Rightarrow A = \dfrac{F(fs)}{S}\). Mass \(m = \rho A L = F L (fs)\,\dfrac{\rho}{S}\). So the lightest tie has the highest \(\boxed{S/\rho}\).
  2. Iso-rigidity. \(\delta = \dfrac{FL}{AE}\Rightarrow A = \dfrac{FL}{E\delta}\). Mass \(m = \dfrac{F L^2}{\delta}\dfrac{\rho}{E}\). So the lightest tie has the highest \(\boxed{E/\rho}\).
  3. Beam with a square section of side a (bending moment M, the section keeps its shape). Strength: \(\sigma = 6M/a^3\), so \(a \propto S^{-1/3}\), \(A = a^2 \propto S^{-2/3}\), and the index is \(S^{2/3}/\rho\). Stiffness: \(\delta \propto 1/(E a^4)\), so \(a^2 \propto E^{-1/2}\), and the index is \(E^{1/2}/\rho\).

Cost instead of mass: replace ρ by ρ·Cm (cost per kg). The cost ratio is the mass ratio times Cm2/Cm1. Ashby's "cheap, stiff column" index is \(E^{1/2}/(C_m\rho)\).

Read the exponent from the geometry. The more freely the material can "spread out" to resist the load (a beam getting deeper, a shaft getting thicker), the smaller the exponent on S or E. So in bending, low density pays off more than high strength.

3. Worked example: steel vs aluminium alloy

Compare steel (ρ = 7.85 g/cm³, S = 300 N/mm², E = 207 000 N/mm²) with an aluminium alloy (ρ = 2.70, S = 200, E = 71 000). Prices assumed for the example: steel ₹60/kg, aluminium ₹250/kg.
  1. Density ratio \(\rho_2/\rho_1 = 2.70/7.85 = 0.344\). Strength ratio \(S_1/S_2 = 1.5\). Modulus ratio \(E_1/E_2 = 2.92\).
  2. Tie, iso-strength: \(0.344 \times 1.5 = 0.52\). The aluminium tie weighs about half as much.
  3. Tie, iso-rigidity: \(0.344 \times 2.92 = 1.00\). Same weight! \(E/\rho\) is almost identical for steel and aluminium (Ti too). That's why a stiff tie gains nothing from aluminium.
  4. Beam, iso-strength: \(0.344 \times 1.5^{2/3} = 0.45\). Beam, iso-rigidity: \(0.344\times2.92^{1/2} = 0.59\). In bending, aluminium saves 40–55% of the weight.
  5. Cost, stiff beam: \(0.59 \times 250/60 = 2.45\). Lighter, but about 2.5 times the material cost. The designer has to judge whether the weight saving is worth it (aircraft: yes; machine-tool bed: no).

4. Weighted point method (Bhandari §2.21)

Screen with go/no-go parameters first. Then for each discriminating property, take each material's share of the column total, multiply by the property's weight (1–5), and add. For cost, use 1/cost, since cheaper is better ().

5. Practice

Carbon-fibre composite (ρ = 1.6 g/cm³, E = 70 000 N/mm²) versus steel (ρ = 7.85, E = 207 000). Find \(m_{CFRP}/m_{steel}\) for a tie of equal stiffness.

\((\rho_2/\rho_1)(E_1/E_2) = (1.6/7.85)(207/70) = 0.204\times2.957 = 0.603\).

The same two materials, for a square-section beam of equal stiffness. Find the mass ratio.

\(0.204\times(2.957)^{1/2} = 0.204\times1.720 = 0.350\). The composite beam weighs about a third of the steel one. In bending, low density is rewarded even more.

Bhandari Example 2.1 (): weights are 5 for UTS, 3 for hardenability, 2 for cost. UTS of the four candidates: 850, 850, 1200, 950. Hardenability: 60, 80, 30, 100. Cost: 40, 50, 100, 80. Find the total points for plain carbon steel (UTS 850, hardenability 80, cost 50).

UTS: 850/3850 × 5 = 1.10. Hardenability: 80/270 × 3 = 0.89. Cost: (1/50)/(1/40+1/50+1/100+1/80) × 2 = 0.02/0.0675 × 2 = 0.59.

Total ≈ 2.58. Chromium steel wins with 2.715.

For a minimum-weight tie that must not stretch more than a set amount, which index do you maximise?

  1. S / ρ for equal strength
  2. E / ρ for equal stiffness
  3. E1/2 / ρ for equal stiffness
  4. S2/3 / ρ for equal strength
A stretch limit is a stiffness (iso-rigidity) constraint. For a tie, \(m = (FL^2/\delta)(\rho/E)\), so maximise E/ρ. E1/2/ρ is the beam version.

In Ashby's method, what is the role of "screening"?

  1. Rank materials by their performance index
  2. Derive the objective function for mass
  3. Remove materials failing go/no-go limits
  4. Test prototypes before final selection
Screening removes materials that violate an absolute constraint (temperature, corrosion, process). Ranking by index comes next, and supporting information (prototypes, supplier data) comes last.

Exam traps

  • Using S/ρ for a beam. The index changes with the geometry. Always derive it from the free variable.
  • Saying "aluminium is lighter so it's better". For a stiff tie, the weights come out equal.
  • In the weighted point method, scoring cost directly. Use 1/cost.
