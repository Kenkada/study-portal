Part B: name the method (GATE-style)

A motor drives a mill through a gearbox. Where should the friction clutch go, and why?

  1. On the mill shaft, where speed is low and shocks are small
  2. Anywhere, since the clutch torque is the same everywhere
  3. Between motor and gearbox, where torque is lowest
  4. Between gearbox and mill, where torque is highest
Same power at higher speed means lower torque, so a smaller and cheaper clutch ().

A shaft must keep two gears in accurate mesh, and the given data includes a twist limit in degrees per metre. What do you do?

  1. Size it for both strength and rigidity and take the larger diameter
  2. Size it for strength only, since rigidity follows automatically
  3. Size it for rigidity only, since strength never governs gear shafts
  4. Use the ASME code, since it already includes the twist limit
Ex. 9.9: strength gave 33 mm, rigidity 45 mm. Either can govern.

Which arrangement needs the tight side on top?

  1. A horizontal flat-belt drive
  2. A horizontal V-belt drive
  3. A vertical flat-belt drive
  4. A horizontal chain drive
Chains: tight side on top so the slack strand doesn't foul the teeth. Belts: slack side on top to increase the wrap.

Part C: solve (Bhandari Examples)

Ex. 9.8 (): the Ex. 9.5 shaft (Mt = 381 972 N·mm over 800 mm between gear and pulley) with a twist limit of 3°/m; G = 79 300. Diameter on rigidity?

θ = 3 × 0.8 = 2.4°. d⁴ = 584(381 972)(800)/(79 300 × 2.4) ⇒ d = 31.12 mm (strength gave 48.5, so strength governs here).

Ex. 13.6 (): cast-iron pulley (FG 150, fs 5), 4 elliptical arms (major = 2 × minor, major axis in the plane of rotation); tensions 750 and 250 N; pulley Ø300, hub Ø60. Minor axis near the hub (half the arms loaded)?

Mt = 500 × 150 = 75 000; P per arm = 2Mt/(RN) = 250 N; arm length to the hub section = 150 − 30 = 120 mm, so Mb = 30 000. With σ = 30 and I = πa⁴/8, y = a: a = 13.66 → 15 mm, b = 30 mm.

Ex. 11.7 (): wet scooter clutch with cork lining, 10 N·m, μ = 0.2, pa = 0.1, d/D = 65/95, uniform wear; add 5% for radial oil slots. Number of contacting surfaces?

P = π(0.1)(65)(30)/2 = 306.3 N; z = 4(10 000)/(0.2 × 306.3 × 160) = 4.08; × 1.05 = 4.28, so 5.

Ex. 12.6 (): short-shoe brake holding a 500 kg load on a 150 mm cable drum; the brake drum (R = 200 mm) turns 4× faster than the cable drum; μ = 0.2. Required shoe force (torque balance through the gearing)?

Same power: n₁(500 × 9.81 × 150) = 4n₁(0.2P × 200) ⇒ P = 4598 N. With pv ≤ 2 and p = 1 N/mm²: v = 2 m/s at the brake drum, so the load can be lowered at 0.375 m/s.

Ex. 12.9 (): four-shoe internal brake, w = 50, pmax = 1, μ = 0.3, R = 250, h = C = 200, θ₁ = 15°, θ₂ = 75° (so sin φmax = sin 75°). Total torque?

Mf = 350 082, Mn = 1 355 173 ⇒ P = 5025 N. Leading-shoe torque 686 298 N·mm; trailing p′max = 0.589, so 404 500. Pair: 1.091 × 10⁶; two pairs: 2182 N·m.

Ex. 12.13 (): light two-wheeler, 1500 N·m, 3 calipers × 2 circular pads, μ = 0.35, pav = 2 MPa, R/e = 0.2 (δ = 0.9693). Pad radius?

Per pad 250 N·m. Rf = δe = 0.9693(R/0.2) = 4.8465R. 250 000 = 0.35(2πR²)(4.8465R) ⇒ R = 28.6 → 30 mm.

Ex. 14.7 (): simple chain 10B (rated 11.67 kW at 1400 rpm for 17 teeth), 19-tooth driver (K₂ = 1.11), smooth operation (Ks = 1). Rated power the drive can be recommended for?

Rearranging Eq. 14.8: kW = rating × K₁ × K₂/Ks = 11.67 × 1 × 1.11/1.0 = 12.95 kW.
