Couplings II: rigid flange and bushed-pin flexible couplings

About 35 minutes · Bhandari §9.21–9.24

Your win: you can derive the bolt equations for a rigid flange coupling (bolts in shear or bolts preloaded for friction), design a complete protected flange coupling, and design a bushed-pin flexible coupling including the pin bending check, which is usually what governs.

1. Rigid flange coupling

Two cast-iron flanges are keyed to the shafts and bolted together on a pitch circle. A spigot and recess align them. The protected type has a rim shrouding the bolt heads for operator safety. For: high torque, simple, easy to assemble. Against: no misalignment tolerance, no shock absorption, large radial size ().

Standard proportions (Fig. 9.35)

Hub dh = 2d · hub length lh = 1.5d · bolt PCD D = 3d · flange thickness t = 0.5d · rim t₁ = 0.25d · spigot dr = 1.5d · outer D₀ = 4d + 2t₁ · bolts N = 3 (d ≤ 40), 4 (40–100), 6 (100–180)

Derive the bolt-size equations for the two ways bolts can transmit torque (Eqs 9.41–9.47).
  1. Case I: bolts fitted in reamed holes (finger tight, they act in shear). Each bolt carries P at radius D/2: \(M_t = N\,P\,\dfrac{D}{2}\), with \(P = \dfrac{\pi}{4}d_1^2\tau\). So \(\boxed{\tau = \dfrac{8M_t}{\pi D N d_1^2}}\). The bolt sees direct shear, not torsion.
  2. Crushing of the bolt in the flange (bearing length = flange thickness t): \(M_t = N\,(d_1 t\sigma_c)\,\dfrac{D}{2}\Rightarrow \sigma_c = \dfrac{2M_t}{N d_1 t D}\).
  3. Case II: bolts in clearance holes, preloaded Pi (friction between flanges). Normal force N·Pi; friction acts at the friction radius of the contact annulus: \(R_f = \dfrac{2}{3}\dfrac{R_o^3 - R_i^3}{R_o^2 - R_i^2}\) (uniform pressure). So \(\boxed{M_t = \mu P_i N R_f}\), and the bolt is sized in tension: \(P_i = \frac{\pi}{4}d_1^2\sigma_t\).
  4. Flange checks: hub as a hollow shaft, \(\tau = 16M_td_h/\pi(d_h^4 - d^4)\); flange shearing off the hub along the cylinder πdh·t at radius dh/2: \(M_t = \pi d_h t\,\tau\,\dfrac{d_h}{2}\Rightarrow \tau = \dfrac{2M_t}{\pi d_h^2 t}\).

2. Bushed-pin flexible coupling

Like a flange coupling, but the bolts become pins fixed in the output flange, carrying rubber bushes (brass-lined) in the input flange. There is a gap between the flanges. It tolerates misalignment (about 0.5 mm lateral or axial, 1.5° angular), absorbs shock and vibration, and carries high torque. It costs more and is bigger (). Why misalignment exists: shaft deflection, mounting tolerances, separately built units (motor + gearbox), thermal expansion.

Equations (Eqs 9.48–9.52) \[ M_t = N\,P\,\frac{D}{2},\quad P = p_m D_b l_b\ (p_m \approx 1\ \text{N/mm}^2,\ l_b = D_b) \Rightarrow D_b^2 = \frac{2M_t}{DN} \] \[ \tau_{pin} = \frac{8M_t}{\pi d_1^2 D N} \le 35\ \text{N/mm}^2 \qquad d_1 \approx \frac{0.5d}{\sqrt N} \qquad \sigma_b = \frac{32\,P\,(\text{gap} + l_b/2)}{\pi d_1^3} \]

Proportions: dh = 2d, lh = 1.5d, D = 3d–4d, t = 0.5d, t₁ = 0.25d. The pin is a cantilever from the output flange, with the bush load P spread over lb, so the arm to the section is gap + lb/2. Peripheral speed ≤ 30 m/s.

Why a flexible coupling has a bigger bolt circle or more pins than a rigid one for the same shaft: the rubber can only take about 1 N/mm², so the force per pin must be small, which needs a larger D or more pins (). Place couplings close to bearings to keep shaft bending small.

3. Worked example (Bhandari Ex. 9.22): complete rigid coupling

Example 9.22 (). 37.5 kW at 180 rpm, service factor 1.5. Shaft 40C8 (Syt = 380, fs 2.5); keys and bolts 30C8 (Syt = 400, Syc = 1.5Syt, fs 2.5); flanges FG 200 (fs 6 on Ssu = 0.5Sut).
  1. Allowables: shaft τ = 76; keys and bolts τ = 80, σc = 240; flange τ = 16.67 N/mm².
  2. Mt = 1.5 × 60×10⁶ × 37.5/(2π × 180) = 2 984 155 N·mm ⇒ d = 58.5, so 60 mm.
  3. Proportions: dh = 120, lh = 90, D = 180, t = 30, t₁ = 15, dr = 90, D₀ = 270 mm.
  4. Hub: τ = Mt(60)/[π(120⁴ − 60⁴)/32] = 9.38 ✓. Flange at hub: 2Mt/(π × 120² × 30) = 4.40 ✓.
  5. Bolts (N = 4): d₁² = 8Mt/(π × 180 × 4 × 80) ⇒ d₁ = 11.5, so 12 mm. Crushing 2Mt/(4 × 12 × 30 × 180) = 23.0 ✓.
  6. Key 18 × 11 × 90: τ = 61.4 ✓, σc = 201 ✓.

4. Practice (Bhandari Examples)

Ex. 9.20 (): rigid coupling, 20 kW at 720 rpm, 4 bolts on a 125 mm PCD in reamed holes, 45C8 (Syt = 380), (fs) = 3, distortion energy theory. Bolt diameter?

τ = 0.577(380)/3 = 73.09; Mt = 265 258. d₁² = 8(265 258)/(π × 125 × 4 × 73.09) ⇒ d₁ = 4.3 mm (use a practical minimum such as M8).

Ex. 9.21 (): 50 kW at 300 rpm, 6 bolts in clearance holes, flange OD 200, recess Ø150, μ = 0.15, 45C8 (fs 3). Bolt core diameter?

Mt = 1 591 549; Rf = (2/3)(100³ − 75³)/(100² − 75²) = 88.1 mm. Pi = Mt/(μNRf) = 20 073 N. d₁ = √(4Pi/(π × 126.67)) = 14.2 mm.

Ex. 9.23 (): flexible coupling, 15 kW at 100 rpm, 6 pins on a 200 mm PCD; bush length 35, gap 5 mm; τ = 35, σb = 152. Pin diameter from bending?

Mt = 1 432 394; P = 2Mt/(DN) = 2387 N. Mb = P(5 + 35/2) = 2387 × 22.5. 152 = 32Mb/πd₁³ ⇒ d₁ = 15.33 mm (shear needs only 9.32 mm).

Ex. 9.24 (): bushed-pin coupling for a 20 kW, 720 rpm motor; starting torque 1.5×; d = 30, D = 4d = 120, 6 pins; pin σb = 200; rubber pm = 1, lb = Db; gap 5 mm. Pin diameter from bending (with lb = 35)?

Mt = 397 887; Db = √(2Mt/(DN)) = 33.2, so 35 mm. P = 2Mt/(DN) = 1105 N. Mb = 1105 × 22.5 ⇒ d₁ = 10.82, so 12 mm. The empirical 0.5d/√N = 6.1 → 7 mm passes shear (28.7 < 35) but fails bending. Bending governs.

Ex. 9.25 (): multiflex coupling, 15 kW at 720 rpm, coils 4 × 2 mm at R = 30 mm, FeE 220, (fs) 2.5, distortion energy theory; each coil is in double shear. Number of coils?

τ = 0.577(220)/2.5 = 50.78. Mt = 2Aτ N R ⇒ N = 198 944/(2 × 8 × 50.78 × 30) = 8.2, so 9 coils.

5. GATE corner

The bolts of a rigid flange coupling fitted in reamed holes are subjected to:

  1. Direct shear stress and crushing on their shanks
  2. Torsional shear stress about their own axes
  3. Pure tensile stress from the bolt preload
  4. Bending stress from the gap between flanges
Each bolt carries a tangential force P = 2Mt/(ND): direct shear on the shank and bearing on the flange. No torque acts about the bolt axis ().

In a bushed-pin flexible coupling, the pin diameter is usually decided by:

  1. Bearing pressure on the brass lining
  2. Direct shear at 35 N/mm² (IS 2693)
  3. Bending as a cantilever across the gap
  4. Crushing of the cast-iron output flange
In both Bhandari examples, bending gives the larger diameter (15.3 vs 9.3 mm; 10.8 vs 7 mm).

For a flange coupling with bolts in clearance holes, the torque capacity is proportional to:

  1. the bolt shear area and the pitch circle radius
  2. the bolt preload, μ and the friction radius
  3. the flange thickness and the hub diameter
  4. the key length and the shaft diameter
Mt = μ Pi N Rf: friction between the flanges, not bolt shear.

Exam traps

  • Using torsional shear for the coupling bolts. They take direct shear.
  • Forgetting the service or starting-torque factor (1.5) before sizing.
  • Stopping at the pin shear check in a flexible coupling.
