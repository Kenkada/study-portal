Unit 4 · Lesson 41

Leaf (multi-leaf) springs

About 35 minutes · Bhandari §10.16–10.17

Your win: you can model the graduated leaves as a triangular plate and the extra full-length leaves as a rectangular cantilever. From that you can derive how the load divides between them, prove that the full-length leaves are stressed 50% more, and derive the nip and pre-load that equalise the stresses. Then you can size the leaves of a vehicle spring.
Warm-up (Lesson 1 and SOM)
Bending stress in a rectangular section b × t under moment M?
σ = My/I = 6M/bt².
Tip deflection of a uniform cantilever with end load P?
δ = PL³/3EI.
What is a beam of uniform strength?
One whose section varies so that M/Z, and hence the stress, is the same at every section.

1. Construction

A semi-elliptic spring is a stack of flat leaves of graduated lengths. The longest, the master leaf, is rolled into eyes at both ends to bolt to the chassis. One or two extra full-length leaves sit under it to carry the transverse shear. U-bolts and a centre clip hold the stack to the axle, and rebound clips keep the leaves aligned. Used on cars, trucks and railway wagons ().

The axle pushes up with 2P at the centre and each eye carries P, so each half acts as a cantilever of length L (half the eye-to-eye distance) with P at its end. Notation: nf extra full-length leaves, ng graduated leaves including the master leaf, n = nf + ng, leaf width b and thickness t.

2. Derivation: load sharing and the 50% stress penalty (Eqs 10.36–10.38)

Derive the stresses in the graduated and full-length leaves of an un-nipped spring, and its end deflection.
  1. Graduated leaves = triangular plate carrying Pg. At the root Imax = ngbt³/12, so \((\sigma_b)_g = \dfrac{6P_gL}{n_gbt^2}\).
  2. In a triangular plate, M and I both grow linearly from the tip, so M/EI (the curvature) is constant along the length. A constant curvature 1/ρ = PgL/EImax over length L gives a tip deflection L²/2ρ: \(\delta_g = \dfrac{P_gL^3}{2EI_{max}} = \dfrac{6P_gL^3}{En_gbt^3}\).
  3. Full-length leaves = rectangular cantilever of width nfb carrying Pf: \((\sigma_b)_f = \dfrac{6P_fL}{n_fbt^2}\) and \(\delta_f = \dfrac{P_fL^3}{3EI} = \dfrac{4P_fL^3}{En_fbt^3}\).
  4. The leaves are clamped together, so δg = δf: \(\dfrac{6P_g}{n_g} = \dfrac{4P_f}{n_f}\Rightarrow P_g = \dfrac{2n_g}{3n_f}P_f\). With Pg + Pf = P: \(P_f = \dfrac{3n_fP}{3n_f + 2n_g}\) and \(P_g = \dfrac{2n_gP}{3n_f + 2n_g}\).
  5. Substitute: \(\boxed{(\sigma_b)_g = \dfrac{12PL}{(3n_f + 2n_g)bt^2}}\) and \(\boxed{(\sigma_b)_f = \dfrac{18PL}{(3n_f + 2n_g)bt^2}}\). The full-length leaves carry 50% more stress.
  6. End deflection: \(\boxed{\delta = \dfrac{12PL^3}{Ebt^3(3n_f + 2n_g)}}\) (Eq. 10.38). It holds with or without nipping.

3. Derivation: nipping equalises the stresses (§10.17)

Before assembly, the full-length leaves are bent to a larger radius than the graduated leaves. That leaves a gap C, the nip, between them at the ends. Tightening the centre clip closes the gap and pre-stresses the leaves in opposite senses. Under full load, all the leaves then end up at the same stress. Nipping is standard in automobile springs ().

Derive the equal stress, the nip C and the pre-load Pi (Eqs 10.39–10.41).
  1. Equal stresses: \(\dfrac{6P_gL}{n_gbt^2} = \dfrac{6P_fL}{n_fbt^2}\Rightarrow \dfrac{P_g}{P_f} = \dfrac{n_g}{n_f}\), so \(P_g = \dfrac{n_g}{n}P\) and \(P_f = \dfrac{n_f}{n}P\). The load now splits in proportion to the number of leaves.
  2. Common stress: \(\boxed{\sigma_b = \dfrac{6PL}{nbt^2}}\) (Eq. 10.41). This is the design equation for nipped springs.
  3. At full load the graduated leaves deflect more than the full-length leaves by exactly C: \(C = \dfrac{6P_gL^3}{En_gbt^3} - \dfrac{4P_fL^3}{En_fbt^3} = \dfrac{6PL^3}{Enbt^3} - \dfrac{4PL^3}{Enbt^3} = \boxed{\dfrac{2PL^3}{Enbt^3}}\).
  4. Pre-load to close the nip: treat it as Pi/2 bending each group towards the other, so \(C = \dfrac{6(P_i/2)L^3}{En_gbt^3} + \dfrac{4(P_i/2)L^3}{En_fbt^3}\). Solving: \(\boxed{P_i = \dfrac{2n_gn_fP}{n(3n_f + 2n_g)}}\) (Eq. 10.40).
  • Materials: 55Si2Mn90, 50Cr1 or 50Cr1V23, hardened and tempered. (fs) on yield is 2–2.5 for vehicle suspension ().
  • Standard sizes (IS 1135). Thickness: 3.2, 4.5, 5, 6, 6.5, 7, 7.5, 8, 9, 10, 11, 12, 14, 16 mm. Width: 32, 40, 45, 50, 55, 60, 65, 70, 75, 80, 90, 100, 125 mm.

Your professor's slides add one idea (Khurmi style, p. 772). The U-bolts clamp a central band of width l so rigidly that this part does not bend. The effective length is then 2L = (span) − l, and L is half of that. Bhandari's examples take L as half the eye-to-eye distance. Use the band rule whenever a question gives a U-bolt spacing.

4. Worked example (Bhandari Ex. 10.25)

Ex. 10.25 (): truck rear spring, nf = 2, ng = 10, eyes 1.2 m apart, 55Si2Mo90 (Syt = 1500, E = 207 000), (fs) = 2.5, maximum force 30 kN. The leaves are pre-stressed to equalise stress. Find the leaf section and the end deflection.
  1. Each cantilever: P = 30/2 = 15 kN at L = 1200/2 = 600 mm. Allowable σ = 1500/2.5 = 600 N/mm². n = 12.
  2. Nipped spring: \(600 = \dfrac{6(15\,000)(600)}{12\,bt^2}\Rightarrow bt^2 = 7500\ \text{mm}^3\).
  3. Pick a standard width, b = 60 mm: t² = 125, so t = 11.18. Take the standard t = 12 mm. Section 60 × 12 mm.
  4. \(\delta = \dfrac{12(15\,000)(600)^3}{207\,000(60)(12)^3(3\times2 + 2\times10)} = \mathbf{69.68\ mm}\).

5. Practice (Bhandari Examples)

Ex. 10.24 (, also slides p. 774): automobile spring, nf = 3, ng = 15, eyes 1 m apart, maximum force 75 kN, b/t = 9, E = 207 000, nipped so that every leaf is at 450 N/mm² under full load. Leaf thickness required (before rounding)?

P = 37 500 N, L = 500 mm, n = 18. 450 = 6(37 500)(500)/[18(9t)t²], so t³ = 1543.2 and t = 11.56 → 12 mm. Then b = 9t = 108 mm.

Ex. 10.24 continued (t = 12, b = 108): the initial nip C?

C = 2PL³/(Enbt³) = 2(37 500)(500)³/[207 000(18)(108)(12)³] = 13.48 mm.

Ex. 10.24 continued: the pre-load needed to close the nip?

Pi = 2ngnfP/[n(3nf + 2ng)] = 2(15)(3)(37 500)/[18(9 + 30)] = 4807.7 N.

Application on Ex. 10.25's spring (60 × 12 leaves, nf = 2, ng = 10, P = 15 kN, L = 600): suppose it were assembled without nipping. What stress would the full-length leaves see?

(σ)f = 18PL/[(3nf + 2ng)bt²] = 18(15 000)(600)/[26(60)(144)] = 721.2 N/mm², above the 600 allowed, while the graduated leaves see 480.8. Nipping brings every leaf to 6PL/nbt² = 520.8. That is the whole case for nipping in one number.

6. GATE corner

In an un-nipped multi-leaf spring, the stress in the extra full-length leaves compared with the graduated leaves is:

  1. equal, since all leaves bend together
  2. 50% greater than in the graduated leaves
  3. 50% smaller than in the graduated leaves
  4. twice as large as in the graduated leaves
σf/σg = 18/12 = 1.5 (Eqs 10.36–10.37).

The graduated leaves of a leaf spring are modelled as a triangular plate because it is:

  1. the lightest possible shape for any spring
  2. a beam with maximum deflection at its root
  3. a shape that removes all transverse shear
  4. a cantilever of uniform strength in bending
Width ∝ distance from the tip, so M/Z is constant along the length (Fig. 10.32).

"Nipping" of a leaf spring means:

  1. pre-stressing by bending leaves to different radii
  2. clamping the leaves with U-bolts at the centre
  3. grinding the leaf ends to reduce stress concentration
  4. adding rebound clips to stop the leaves shifting sideways
The full-length leaves get a larger radius of curvature. The gap C closes when the centre bolt is tightened (§10.17).

Exam traps

  • The force in the formulas is the end load P, half the centre load 2P. L is half the effective length.
  • ng includes the master leaf.
  • Nipped spring: σ = 6PL/nbt². Un-nipped: use 12PL or 18PL over (3nf + 2ng)bt². The deflection formula is the same for both.
  • Round t and b to the standard sizes, then recompute deflection with the rounded values.
  • If a U-bolt spacing is given (slides, Khurmi style), subtract it from the span before halving.
