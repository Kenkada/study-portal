Shafts with pulleys and gears: the ASME code

About 35 minutes · Bhandari §9.4

Your win: you can turn belt tensions and gear-tooth forces into vertical and horizontal bending-moment diagrams, find the critical section, and size the shaft by the ASME code with shock factors kb, kt. This is the most common long shaft question.
Warm-up (Lesson 25)

1. Derivation: what a pulley and a gear do to a shaft

Derive the forces and torque a belt pulley and a spur gear apply to their shaft.
  1. Pulley of radius R with tight and slack tensions P₁, P₂: torque Mt = (P₁ − P₂)R. The shaft feels a transverse pull (P₁ + P₂) along the belt direction, plus the pulley weight acting downward.
  2. Belt tension ratio for wrap angle θ and friction μ: P₁/P₂ = eμθ (derived in Lesson 30). With Mt known, solve P₁ − P₂ = Mt/R together with P₁/P₂ = eμθ.
  3. Spur gear of pitch diameter d′ with pressure angle α: tangential force Pt = 2Mt/d′ (gives torque and bending in the tangential plane); radial force Pr = Pt tan α (towards the gear centre, in the plane of the centres).
  4. Resolve every force into a vertical and a horizontal plane. Find the bearing reactions and BMD in each plane. At every section: \(M_b = \sqrt{M_V^2 + M_H^2}\).
  5. The critical section has the largest combination of Mb and Mt. Usually it's the largest Mb, since Mt is constant between the driving and driven elements.

2. The ASME code (§9.4)

Eqs 9.14, 9.15 \[ \tau_{max} = \min(0.30\,S_{yt},\ 0.18\,S_{ut}),\ \ \times 0.75 \text{ if there is a keyway} \] \[ \tau_{max} = \frac{16}{\pi d^3}\sqrt{(k_bM_b)^2 + (k_tM_t)^2} \]
Combined shock and fatigue factors, rotating shafts (Table 9.2, )

Gradually applied1.51.0

Why kb ≥ 1.5 even for steady load: a rotating shaft under a steady transverse load sees fully reversed bending stress every revolution (Lesson 6). The factor stands in for fatigue. The ASME code is the maximum shear stress theory with these factors.

3. Worked example (Bhandari Ex. 9.5): gear plus overhung pulley

Example 9.5 (). Bearings A and C are 800 mm apart. Spur gear B (pitch Ø300, 20°) is midway between them; pulley D (Ø450) overhangs C by 400 mm with vertical belts, P₁ = 3P₂. 20 kW at 500 rpm passes from pulley to gear. Gear forces: Pt horizontal, Pr vertical downward. Both keyed. 50C4 (Sut = 700, Syt = 460), kb = kt = 1.5.
  1. Allowable: min(0.30 × 460 = 138, 0.18 × 700 = 126) = 126, × 0.75 for keyways = 94.5 N/mm².
  2. Mt = 60×10⁶ × 20/(2π × 500) = 381 972 N·mm.
  3. Pulley: (P₁ − P₂)(225) = Mt and P₁ = 3P₂ ⇒ P₁ = 2546.5, P₂ = 848.8; belt pull 3395.3 N downward at D.
  4. Gear: Pt = Mt/150 = 2546.5 N (horizontal); Pr = 2546.5 tan 20° = 926.8 N (vertical).
  5. Vertical plane: moments about A give RC = (926.8 × 400 + 3395.3 × 1200)/800 = 5556.4 N, so RA = −1234.2 N. MV at C = 3395.3 × 400 = 1 358 124; at B = −493 692 N·mm.
  6. Horizontal plane: RA = RC = 1273.2 N; MH at B = 509 296 N·mm, zero at C.
  7. Resultant: B: √(493 692² + 509 296²) = 709 300; C: 1 358 124 N·mm. C is critical.
  8. \(d^3 = \frac{16}{\pi(94.5)}\sqrt{(1.5\times1\,358\,124)^2 + (1.5\times381\,972)^2}\Rightarrow d = 48.5\) mm.

4. Practice (Bhandari Examples)

Ex. 9.3 (): line shaft, pulleys keyed, 40C8 (Sut = 650, Syt = 380), kb = 1.5, kt = 1.0. Maximum belt tension 2.7 kN on the smaller pulley A (Ø250, ratio 3:1). The resultant moment at A works out to 847 703 N·mm. Diameter?

Allowable: min(114, 117) = 114, × 0.75 = 85.5. Mt = (2700 − 900)(125) = 225 000.

\(d^3 = \frac{16}{\pi(85.5)}\sqrt{(1.5 \times 847\,703)^2 + 225\,000^2}\Rightarrow d = 42.53\) mm. (The smaller pulley always carries the larger tension for a given torque.)

Ex. 9.4 (): gearbox intermediate shaft, keyed gears, FeE 580 (Sut = 770, Syt = 580), kb = 1.5, kt = 2.0. Critical resultant Mb = 3 498 327 N·mm, Mt = 1 989 450 N·mm. Diameter?

Allowable: min(174, 138.6) × 0.75 = 103.95. \(d^3 = \frac{16}{\pi(103.95)}\sqrt{(1.5\times3\,498\,327)^2 + (2\times1\,989\,450)^2}\Rightarrow d = 68.59\) mm.

Ex. 9.6 (): helical gear plus overhung bevel gear, keyed, 45C8 (Sut = 600, Syt = 380), kb = 2.0, kt = 1.5. Largest Mb = 263 284 N·mm, Mt = 144 000 N·mm. Diameter?

Allowable: min(114, 108) × 0.75 = 81. \(d^3 = \frac{16}{\pi(81)}\sqrt{(2\times263\,284)^2 + (1.5\times144\,000)^2}\Rightarrow d = 32.95\) mm. Rigidity later raises this to 45.4 mm (Ex. 9.9).

Ex. 9.7 (): armature shaft of a 40 kW, 720 rpm motor. A 7 kN magnetic pull is spread over the middle 700 mm of a 1000 mm bearing span, and a keyed pulley overhangs bearing B by 300 mm with a 4 kN total belt pull, giving Mb = 1.2 × 10⁶ N·mm at B. Sut = 770, Syt = 580, kb = 1.5, kt = 1.0. Diameter?

Mt = 530 516 N·mm; allowable 103.95. \(d^3 = \frac{16}{\pi(103.95)}\sqrt{(1.8\times10^6)^2 + 530\,516^2}\Rightarrow d = 45.13\) mm.

Ex. 9.1 (), MSST without ASME factors: pulley B (Ø500, vertical belt, Pmax = 2.5 kN, μ = 0.24, θ = 180°) drives pulley C (Ø250, horizontal belt). 30C8, (fs) = 3. The resultant moment at C is 1 185 625 N·mm. Diameter?

e0.24π = 2.125, so P₂ = 1176.5 and Mt = (2500 − 1176.5)(250) = 330 883. τ = 66.67.

\(d^3 = \frac{16}{\pi(66.67)}\sqrt{1\,185\,625^2 + 330\,883^2}\Rightarrow d = 45.47\) mm. (Pulley C: P₃ = 5000, P₄ = 2353 N. The smaller pulley carries higher tensions.)

5. GATE corner

A spur gear (pressure angle 20°) transmits a tangential force of 2 kN. The radial force on the shaft is about:

  1. 0.68 kN, which is 2 sin 20°
  2. 1.88 kN, which is 2 cos 20°
  3. 0.73 kN, which is 2 tan 20°
  4. 5.49 kN, which is 2 / tan 20°
Pr = Pt tan α = 2 × 0.364 = 0.728 kN. The total tooth force is Pt/cos α = 2.13 kN.

Why is the bending moment multiplied by kb ≥ 1.5 in the ASME code even for steady loads?

  1. Bending stress fully reverses each revolution
  2. The yield strength in bending is lower
  3. Keyways always reduce bending strength
  4. Belt tensions are never known exactly
A point on a rotating shaft passes from tension to compression every turn, which is fatigue loading. The factor covers it in a static-looking formula.

Two pulleys on one shaft carry the same torque. The belt tensions are higher on:

  1. the larger pulley, which has more wrap
  2. whichever pulley has the vertical belt
  3. both pulleys equally, since torque is equal
  4. the smaller pulley, with the shorter arm
(P₁ − P₂) = Mt/R: a smaller R needs larger tensions, so the bigger bending load comes from the smaller pulley (Bhandari Ex. 9.3).

Exam traps

  • Forgetting the 0.75 keyway factor, or taking the larger of 0.30Syt and 0.18Sut (take the smaller).
  • Adding vertical and horizontal moments directly. Combine them vectorially at the same section.
  • Ignoring the pulley's own weight when it is given.
