Flat belt drives: geometry, tensions and power

1. Flexible drives and belts: when and why

Flexible drives (belt, rope, chain) span long distances, absorb shock, tolerate loose centre-distance tolerances and are cheap. On the other hand they take more space, give smaller velocity ratios (flat belt up to 4:1, V-belt 7:1, chain 15:1), and the ratio isn't exact (). Belts also slip under overload, which protects the machine, run quietly, and are simple, but they load the bearings heavily and wear out sooner.

2. Derivation: wrap angle and belt length

Derive Bhandari Eqs 13.1–13.5 for pulleys D (big) and d (small) at centre distance C.
  1. Open belt. The straight belt span is tangent to both pulleys. Drop a perpendicular from the small-pulley centre onto the big-pulley radius: the triangle has hypotenuse C and opposite side (D − d)/2, so \(\sin\beta = \dfrac{D-d}{2C}\).
  2. Wrap angles: \(\alpha_s = 180° - 2\beta\) (small pulley), \(\alpha_b = 180° + 2\beta\) (big pulley). The small pulley governs, because its wrap is smaller.
  3. Length = two arcs + two straight spans: \(L = \frac{d}{2}(\pi - 2\beta) + \frac{D}{2}(\pi + 2\beta) + 2C\cos\beta = \frac{\pi}{2}(D+d) + \beta(D-d) + 2C\cos\beta\).
  4. For small β: β ≈ (D − d)/2C and cos β ≈ 1 − β²/2. Substitute and simplify: \(\boxed{L = 2C + \dfrac{\pi}{2}(D+d) + \dfrac{(D-d)^2}{4C}}\).
  5. Crossed belt: \(\sin\beta = \dfrac{D+d}{2C}\), both wraps = 180° + 2β, and \(L = 2C + \frac{\pi}{2}(D+d) + \frac{(D+d)^2}{4C}\). The crossed belt reverses rotation and has more wrap (more power), but it rubs on itself and bends in two planes (more wear).

3. Derivation: tension ratio with centrifugal tension

Derive \(\dfrac{P_1 - mv^2}{P_2 - mv^2} = e^{f\alpha}\) (Eq. 13.6) for a belt of mass m per metre at speed v.
  1. Take a small element subtending dφ. Forces: tensions P and P + dP at its ends; normal reaction dN; friction f·dN along the belt; centrifugal force = (mass)(v²/r) = (m r dφ)(v²/r) = m v² dφ, outward.
  2. Tangential equilibrium (cos(dφ/2) ≈ 1): \((P + dP) - P = f\,dN\Rightarrow dN = dP/f\).
  3. Radial equilibrium (sin(dφ/2) ≈ dφ/2): \(dN + mv^2d\phi = (P + dP)\frac{d\phi}{2} + P\frac{d\phi}{2}\approx P\,d\phi\) (dropping dP·dφ).
  4. Substitute dN: \(\dfrac{dP}{f} = (P - mv^2)\,d\phi\Rightarrow \dfrac{dP}{P - mv^2} = f\,d\phi\).
  5. Integrate from P₂ to P₁ and from 0 to α: \(\ln\dfrac{P_1 - mv^2}{P_2 - mv^2} = f\alpha\). With mv² negligible (slow belts): \(P_1/P_2 = e^{f\alpha}\).
  6. Centrifugal tension on its own (§13.6): an element in equilibrium has 2Pc(dφ/2) = mv²dφ, so \(P_c = mv^2\). It adds equally to both sides and takes no part in transmitting power.

4. Derivation: condition for maximum power

Derive Pmax = 3Pc and the optimum speed (§13.6).
  1. The belt is limited to Pmax = (width × thickness × allowable stress). The tight-side tension available for power is P₁ = Pmax − mv².
  2. Power = (P₁ − P₂)v = P₁(1 − e−fα)v = k(Pmax − mv²)v, where k = 1 − 1/efα.
  3. Maximise: \(\dfrac{d}{dv}(P_{max}v - mv^3) = P_{max} - 3mv^2 = 0\).
  4. So \(\boxed{v_{opt} = \sqrt{P_{max}/3m}}\), \(\boxed{P_{max} = 3P_c}\), and P₁ = 2Pc.
  5. Initial tension (equal stretch on tight side and contraction on slack side): \(P_i = \tfrac12(P_1 + P_2)\). An equivalent form: \(v_{opt} = \sqrt{P_i/3m}\) (Eq. 13.10).

5. Belt behaviour: creep, slip, sag, the law of belting

  • Creep: the belt shortens elastically as it moves from tight to slack side, so it creeps on the pulley. The driven speed drops slightly and efficiency falls by 1–2%. Slip: the whole belt slides over the whole arc under overload ().
  • Keep the slack side on top in horizontal drives (sag increases the wrap). Avoid vertical drives.
  • Law of belting: the centre line of the belt approaching a pulley must lie in that pulley's mid-plane. So a quarter-turn drive runs in one direction only.
  • Crowning one pulley keeps the belt centred. Pulley arms are elliptical with the major axis in the plane of rotation (less air drag, less material): \(a = 1.72\sqrt[3]{M_t/(N\sigma_b)}\) with half the arms taking load.
  • Centre distance guide: C = D + 1.5d (ratio < 3), C = D (ratio > 3).

6. Worked example (Bhandari Ex. 13.1): design from first principles

Example 13.1 (). A 15 kW leather belt drive from 1440 to 480 rpm; v ≈ 20 m/s; σ ≤ 2.25 N/mm²; ρ = 0.95 g/cc; μ = 0.35; t = 5 mm; C = 2D.
  1. d = 60 000v/πn = 265 → 270 mm; D = 3d = 810 mm; true v = 20.36 m/s.
  2. C = 1620 mm. L = 2(1620) + (π/2)(1080) + 540²/(4 × 1620) = 4981 mm.
  3. αs = 180 − 2 sin⁻¹(540/3240) = 160.8° = 2.81 rad, so efα = 2.67.
  4. m = ρ × area = (4.75 × 10⁻³)b kg/m, so mv² = 1.97b.
  5. Three equations: P₁ = 2.25(5b) = 11.25b; (P₁ − 1.97b) = 2.67(P₂ − 1.97b); P₁ − P₂ = 15 000/20.36 = 736.7.
  6. Solve: b = 127 → 130 mm; P₁ = 1429 N, P₂ = 692 N.

7. Practice (Bhandari Examples)

Ex. 13.3 (): open V-belt drive, pulleys 150 and 300 mm, C = 1 m, groove 40°, m = 0.25 kg/m, Pmax = 750 N, μ = 0.2. Find the maximum power the belt can transmit.

α = 171.4° = 2.99 rad; efα/sin20° = 5.75. At v = 0: P₂ = 750/5.75 = 130.4, so Pi = 440.2 N. vopt = √(Pi/3m) = 24.23 m/s.

With P₁ + P₂ = 880.4 and (P₁ − 146.8)/(P₂ − 146.8) = 5.75: P₁ = 646.7, P₂ = 233.7, so power = 413 × 24.23 = 10.01 kW. Power is zero again at v = √(Pi/m) = 41.96 m/s.

Ex. 13.4 (): select a Dunlop HI-SPEED flat belt for a 25 kW, 1440 rpm motor driving a compressor at 720 rpm, C = 3 m. Using d = 250, D = 500 (v = 18.85 m/s), Fa = 1.3 and arc factor 1.019, what standard width with 5 plies?

Corrected power = 1.3 × 25 × 1.019 = 33.12 kW. Rating = 0.0118 × 18.85/5.08 = 0.0438 kW per mm per ply. Width × plies = 756, so with 5 plies width = 151.2 → 152 mm. L = 7183 mm.

Ex. 13.5 (): a cast-iron pulley (FG 150, (fs) 5), Ø500, 4 elliptical arms (major = 2 × minor, major axis in the plane of rotation), 10 kW at 720 rpm. Minor axis?

σb = 30; Mt = 132 629. With half the arms loaded: \(a = 1.72\sqrt[3]{M_t/(N\sigma_b)} = 1.72\sqrt[3]{132\,629/120} = 17.78\), so a = 20, b = 40 mm.

8. GATE corner

For maximum power transmission by a belt, the centrifugal tension equals:

  1. one-half of the maximum permissible tension
  2. the tension on the slack side of the belt
  3. one-third of the maximum permissible tension
  4. twice the tension on the tight side of the belt
Pmax = 3Pc (and P₁ = 2Pc), from d/dv[(Pmax − mv²)v] = 0.

The ratio of tight- to slack-side tension in a flat belt (centrifugal tension neglected) is 3 for a 180° wrap. With a 270° wrap it becomes:

  1. 31.5 ≈ 5.2
  2. 4.5
  3. 32 = 9
  4. √3 ≈ 1.73
efα scales exponentially: ef(1.5π) = (efπ)1.5 = 31.5 = 5.2.

Centrifugal tension in a belt:

  1. increases the power the belt can transmit
  2. acts only on the tight side of the belt
  3. depends on the wrap angle and μ
  4. reduces the tension available for power
Pc = mv² acts equally on both sides, independent of μ and α. It uses up part of Pmax, leaving P₁ = Pmax − Pc for power.

Creep in a belt drive is caused by:

  1. overload making the belt slide over the whole arc
  2. elastic change of belt length between the two sides
  3. insufficient initial tension when the drive starts
  4. the centrifugal force lifting the belt off the pulley
Creep is a gradual, unavoidable elastic effect. Slip is a gross sliding under overload.

Exam traps

  • Using the big pulley's wrap angle. The small pulley decides slipping.
  • Angles in degrees inside efα. α must be in radians.
  • Forgetting mv² at belt speeds above about 10 m/s.
