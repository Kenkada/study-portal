1. Where the syllabus words come from

Bhandari defines machine design as the use of scientific principles, technical information and imagination to describe a machine that performs specific functions with maximum economy and efficiency (). The words in your syllabus (need, analysis, conceptual design, technical viability, economic and financial viability) are the steps of the feasibility study, the first phase of Morris Asimow's "morphology of design" (Introduction to Design, 1962; summarised in his biography).

StageWhat you doExample: a hand-operated screw jack for cars
NeedRecognise a real (or latent) need. Often vague at first.Motorists need to lift a car to change a tyre at the roadside.
Analysis (problem definition)Turn the need into specifications: inputs, outputs, space, life, cost, quantity, limits.Lift 10 kN by 300 mm; effort ≤ 150 N; mass ≤ 5 kg; cost ≤ ₹1500; self-locking.
Conceptual design (synthesis)Generate several feasible concepts (mechanisms).Screw jack, scissor jack, hydraulic bottle jack, lever jack.
Technical viabilityCan it be built and made to work with available materials, processes and knowledge? Does analysis show it meets the specs?Square-thread screw: self-locking and simple. Hydraulic: needs seals and precision machining.
Economic viabilityDoes the value to the user exceed the total cost (making, using, maintaining)?The screw jack is cheapest and maintenance-free. The hydraulic jack costs more but lifts with less effort.
Financial viabilityCan the organisation fund development, tooling and production until it earns money back?Existing lathes cut the threads, so tooling cost is small.

Your professor's slides use Shigley's phases: recognition of need → definition of problem → synthesis → analysis and optimisation → evaluation → presentation (slides pp. 16–22). They are the same ideas in different words. If a question asks for "phases of design", give the Shigley list. If it uses the syllabus words, give the table above.

Bhandari's five-step procedure for a machine

Once a concept is chosen, Bhandari lists the working steps (–3):

  1. Product specifications: capacity, life, cost, reliability, dimensions, weight.
  2. Selection of mechanism: compare alternatives on cost, availability of materials and processes.
  3. Layout of configuration: a block diagram of the main elements and the joining methods.
  4. Design of individual components: forces → material → likely failure mode and criterion → dimensions with a factor of safety. This course is mostly this step.
  5. Preparation of drawings: assembly and part drawings with materials, tolerances and surface finish, plus lists of standard and special parts.

Basic requirements of a machine element

Strength, rigidity, wear resistance, minimum dimensions and weight, manufacturability, safety, conformance to standards, reliability, maintainability, minimum life-cycle cost (; slides pp. 30–33). Learn the list. It is often asked as "explain any five".

2. Standards and preferred numbers

A standard limits variety (IS, DIN, ISO). A code specifies analysis, design, testing and erection to reach a stated level of safety, e.g. IS 807 for cranes (). Standard parts are cheaper (mass-produced), interchangeable, reliable, and save design effort.

Preferred numbers (Renard series, IS 1076) are geometric progressions used to choose size ranges ():

Series factors \[ R5: \sqrt[5]{10}=1.58 \quad R10: \sqrt[10]{10}=1.26 \quad R20: \sqrt[20]{10}=1.12 \quad R40: 1.06 \quad R80: 1.03 \]

Derived series R20/4 = every 4th term of R20, so ratio = \(1.122^4 = 1.585\). Range from \(a\) to \(b\) in \(m\) steps: ratio \(f = (b/a)^{1/m}\).

R5 from 1 to 10: 1, 1.6, 2.5, 4, 6.3, 10. R10: 1, 1.25, 1.6, 2, 2.5, 3.15, 4, 5, 6.3, 8, 10. Small sizes differ by small amounts and large sizes by large amounts, so the whole range is covered by the fewest sizes.

3. Practice

Question bank Q7 (): build the derived series R20/4 (100, …, 1000). What is its fourth number?

Ratio \(= (\sqrt[20]{10})^4 = 1.5848\). Series: 100, 158.5 → 160, 251.2 → 250, 398.0 → 400, 630.8 → 630, 999.7 → 1000 (Bhandari Example 1.2).

Question bank Q8: standardise eleven shaft diameters from 100 to 1000 mm. What is the sixth diameter?

\(f = (1000/100)^{1/10} = 10^{1/10}\), which is the R10 series. Diameters: 100, 125, 160, 200, 250, 315, 400, 500, 630, 800, 1000 mm (Example 1.4).

A maker plans five tractor models from 7.5 kW to 75 kW in geometric steps. What is the third model's rating, rounded?

\(f = (75/7.5)^{1/4} = 1.7783\). Third model \(= 7.5(1.7783)^2 = 23.7\), so about 24 kW (Example 1.3).

A team shows a proposed gearbox can be made with existing materials, processes and know-how. Which step is this?

  1. Economic viability of the chosen concept
  2. Technical viability of the chosen concept
  3. Financial viability of the chosen concept
  4. Recognition of need for the concept
Technical viability (Asimow: "physical realisability") asks whether the concept can be built and will work. Economic viability asks whether its value exceeds its cost. Financial viability asks whether the money to develop and produce it can be found.

What distinguishes a code from a standard?

  1. A code is national, a standard international
  2. A code lists only dimensions of parts
  3. A code is optional, a standard compulsory
  4. A code sets procedures to ensure safety
Bhandari: a standard limits variety (sizes, materials, processes); a code specifies analysis, design, manufacture, testing and erection to achieve a specified level of safety, e.g. pressure-vessel and crane codes ().

In Bhandari's procedure, which step selects the failure criterion, such as yield strength or endurance limit?

  1. Design of individual components
  2. Product specifications for the machine
  3. Layout of the selected configuration
  4. Preparation of assembly drawings
Step 4 (design of individual components) covers forces, then material, then likely failure mode and criterion, then dimensions with a factor of safety ().

Exam traps

  • Mixing up economic viability (value vs. cost) and financial viability (can it be funded).
  • Giving only a list. A 5-mark answer needs one line of explanation per stage and a running example.
  • Preferred numbers: round to the standard table values (3.15, not 3.16; 6.3, not 6.31).
