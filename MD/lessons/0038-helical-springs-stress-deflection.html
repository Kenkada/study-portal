Unit 4 · Lesson 38

Closed-coiled helical springs: stress and deflection

About 40 minutes · Bhandari §10.1–10.8

Your win: you can derive the spring stress with the shear-stress factor Ks and the Wahl factor K. You can derive the load–deflection equation δ = 8PD³N/Gd⁴, combine springs in series and parallel, and design a helical compression spring from a force, a deflection and a spring index. That is the whole of a typical 10-mark spring question.
Warm-up (Lessons 1, 4 and 25)
Torsional shear stress in a solid round bar of diameter d under torque T?
τ = 16T/πd³ (maximum at the surface).
Angle of twist of a bar of length L?
θ = TL/GJ, with J = πd⁴/32.
Energy stored by a gradually applied load P that deflects δ?
U = ½Pδ, the area under the load–deflection line.

1. What springs do, and the main types

FunctionApplications
Absorb shock and vibrationvehicle suspension, railway buffers, elevator buffers, machine mounts
Store energyclocks, toys, circuit breakers, starters
Measure forcespring balances and scales
Apply force, control motioncam followers, engine valve return, clutch engagement

Types (–395): helical compression and extension springs (by far the most common), helical torsion springs (door hinges, starters), multi-leaf springs (vehicle suspension), spiral, and Belleville (coned disc) springs. Two facts get asked often:

  • The wire of a compression spring is not in compression, and the wire of an extension spring is not in tension. In both, the wire carries torsional shear. The words describe the whole spring.
  • A spring is closely coiled when the helix angle is small (under about 10°), so each coil lies almost in a plane perpendicular to the axis. Open-coiled springs (helix angle over 10°) come in Lesson 40.

Helical springs are easy and cheap to make, reliable, and their deflection is linear in the load ().

2. Terminology you must use correctly

  • Mean coil diameter D = (Di + Do)/2, so D = Do − d = Di + d. Spring index C = D/d. Keep C between 4 and 12, and between 6 and 9 for cyclic loads. Below 3 the curvature stress is excessive and the wire can crack in coiling. Above 15 the spring buckles and tangles ().
  • Solid length = Ntd (coils touching). Free length = solid length + total axial gap + δmax. The total gap is (Nt − 1) × (gap between coils, 0.5–2 mm), or 15% of δmax. Pitch = free length/(Nt − 1).
  • Active coils N carry load and deflect. End coils sitting on the seat are inactive ():
End stylePlainPlain and groundSquareSquare and groundExtension spring
Active coils NNtNt − ½Nt − 2Nt − 2Nt (all active)

Unless told otherwise, Bhandari assumes square and ground ends, so Nt = N + 2.

3. Derivation: stress in the wire (Eqs 10.4–10.7)

Derive \(\tau = K\dfrac{8PD}{\pi d^3}\) for a closely coiled spring under axial force P.
  1. Free body of the cut coil (figure): the wire section, at distance D/2 from the axis, must carry a torque \(M_t = P\cdot\frac{D}{2}\) and a shear force P.
  2. Torsional shear (maximum at the wire surface): \(\tau_1 = \dfrac{16M_t}{\pi d^3} = \dfrac{8PD}{\pi d^3}\).
  3. Direct shear, taken as uniform: \(\tau_2 = \dfrac{P}{\pi d^2/4} = \dfrac{4P}{\pi d^2} = \dfrac{8PD}{\pi d^3}\cdot\dfrac{0.5d}{D}\).
  4. At the inside of the coil they act in the same direction, so \(\tau = \tau_1 + \tau_2 = \dfrac{8PD}{\pi d^3}\Big(1 + \dfrac{0.5}{C}\Big) = K_s\dfrac{8PD}{\pi d^3}\), with \(\boxed{K_s = 1 + \dfrac{0.5}{C}}\).
  5. Curvature effect: when the straight bar is coiled, the inside fibre is shorter than the outside fibre. The same twist gives it more strain, so stress concentrates on the inside (factor Kc). A M Wahl combined both effects, K = KsKc: \(\boxed{K = \dfrac{4C - 1}{4C - 4} + \dfrac{0.615}{C}}\).
  6. Result (Eq. 10.6 and, with D = Cd, Eq. 10.13): \(\boxed{\tau = K\dfrac{8PD}{\pi d^3} = K\dfrac{8PC}{\pi d^2}}\). Use K for static design. Under fluctuating load, Ks goes on the mean stress and K on the amplitude (Lesson 39).

Wahl factor values worth remembering: C = 5 → 1.3105, C = 6 → 1.2525, C = 8 → 1.184, C = 10 → 1.145. K falls towards 1 as C grows, because the curvature becomes gentle.

4. Derivation: deflection and stiffness (Eqs 10.8–10.10)

Derive \(\delta = \dfrac{8PD^3N}{Gd^4}\) and \(k = \dfrac{Gd^4}{8D^3N}\).
  1. Uncoil the N active coils into a straight bar of length \(l = \pi DN\), loaded by the torque \(M_t = PD/2\) (Bhandari Fig. 10.8).
  2. Twist of the bar: \(\theta = \dfrac{M_t\,l}{GJ} = \dfrac{(PD/2)(\pi DN)}{G(\pi d^4/32)} = \dfrac{16PD^2N}{Gd^4}\).
  3. The load point sits at radius D/2, so for small θ the axial deflection is \(\delta = \theta\cdot\dfrac{D}{2}\Rightarrow \boxed{\delta = \dfrac{8PD^3N}{Gd^4}}\).
  4. Stiffness \(\boxed{k = \dfrac{P}{\delta} = \dfrac{Gd^4}{8D^3N}}\). Check by energy: \(U = \dfrac{M_t^2 l}{2GJ}\), and Castigliano's \(\partial U/\partial P\) gives the same δ. You will reuse this energy route for open-coiled springs.
  5. Consequences: k ∝ d⁴ (double d → 16k), k ∝ 1/D³, k ∝ 1/N. A spring cut in half (N/2) is twice as stiff. Energy stored: \(E = \tfrac12 P\delta\) (Eq. 10.10).

5. Springs in series and in parallel (§10.6)

Combinations \[ \text{series (same force, deflections add): } \frac{1}{k} = \frac{1}{k_1} + \frac{1}{k_2} \qquad \text{parallel (same deflection, forces add): } k = k_1 + k_2 \]

Combinations save space, change the rate at a chosen deflection, or give a fail-safe system (–400). The two buffer springs of a wagon side by side and the eight clutch springs on a pressure plate are both in parallel, so the load is shared.

6. Materials and the allowable stress (§10.7–10.8)

  • Patented and cold-drawn steel wire (0.85–0.95% C) is the most used and the cheapest (cost index 1). Bhandari calls it "an aristocrat among springs". Grades 1–4 run from static to severe service, and music wire is its best quality (cost index 3.5). Oil-hardened and tempered wire (SW, and VW for valves) is the reliable choice for fluctuating loads (cost index 1.5). Alloy Cr–V and Cr–Si wire suits shock loads (cost index 4). Stainless wire resists corrosion (cost index 8.5). G = 81 370 N/mm² for these steels (–402).
  • Sut falls as d grows (Table 10.1). That is why real design is trial and error (Lesson 39). Wire of 6 mm and above is hot-wound to avoid high residual stresses.
  • Factor of safety only about 1.5, because deflections are well defined, an overloaded compression spring just closes solid, extension springs have overload stops, and the material is closely controlled. With Syt = 0.75Sut and Ssy = 0.577Syt: \(\tau = \dfrac{0.577(0.75)S_{ut}}{1.5}\approx \boxed{0.3S_{ut}}\). IS 4454 allows up to 0.5Sut ().

7. The design procedure (§10.8)

  1. Find the maximum force P and the deflection δ (or the rate k) from the application.
  2. Pick the material, then τ = 0.3Sut or 0.5Sut. Assume C (8 for industrial work, 5 for valves and clutches, never under 3), and find K.
  3. d from \(\tau = K\,8PC/\pi d^2\), rounded up. Then D = Cd.
  4. N from δ = 8PD³N/Gd⁴, rounded up. Then Nt = N + 2 (square and ground ends).
  5. Solid length Ntd, actual δ, gap (Nt − 1) × 0.5–2 mm, free length, pitch, actual k.
  6. Buckling check: if free length/D > 2.6, guide the spring in a sleeve or over a rod ().

8. Worked example (Bhandari Ex. 10.1)

Ex. 10.1 (): compression spring, Pmax = 1250 N, δ ≈ 30 mm, C = 6, patented cold-drawn wire Sut = 1090 N/mm², G = 81 370, τ = 0.5Sut. Find d, D, N, Nt, free length and pitch.
  1. τ = 0.5(1090) = 545 N/mm². K = (24 − 1)/(24 − 4) + 0.615/6 = 1.2525.
  2. \(545 = 1.2525\,\dfrac{8(1250)(6)}{\pi d^2}\Rightarrow d = 6.63\), so take d = 7 mm. D = Cd = 42 mm.
  3. \(30 = \dfrac{8(1250)(42)^3N}{81\,370(7)^4}\Rightarrow N = 7.91\), so take N = 8. Square and ground ends give Nt = 10.
  4. Actual δ = 8(1250)(42)³(8)/(81 370 × 7⁴) = 30.34 mm. Solid length = 10 × 7 = 70 mm. With a 1 mm gap: total gap = (10 − 1)(1) = 9 mm.
  5. Free length = 70 + 9 + 30.34 = 109.34, take 110 mm. Pitch = 109.34/9 = 12.15 mm. Buckling check: 110/42 = 2.62, just over 2.6, so a guide rod is advisable. Bhandari does not do this check, but it earns a mark.

9. Practice (Bhandari Examples)

Ex. 10.2 (): force varies 2.5 → 3.5 kN with about 5 mm deflection over that range, C = 5, square and ground ends, Sut = 1050, τ = 0.5Sut, G = 81 370. After rounding d and N, what is the actual spring rate?

τ = 525, K = 1.3105. d from Pmax: 525 = 1.3105 × 8(3500)(5)/πd², so d = 10.55 → 11 mm and D = 55 mm. N from the range: 5 = 8(3500 − 2500)(55)³N/(81 370 × 11⁴), so N = 4.48 → 5. Actual k = 81 370(11)⁴/[8(55)³(5)] = 179.0 N/mm (required 200). Solid 77, δmax 19.55, free length 100 mm.

Ex. 10.5 (): spring balance, 0–1500 N over a 100 mm scale, C = 6, Sut = 1360, τ = 0.5Sut. Number of active coils?

τ = 680: 680 = 1.2525 × 8(1500)(6)/πd², so d = 6.50 → 7 mm and D = 42 mm. 100 = 8(1500)(42)³N/(81 370 × 7⁴), so N = 21.97 → 22. Extension spring: all coils active. Actual k = 14.98 N/mm against 15 required.

Ex. 10.6 (): a 1500 kg wagon at 1.5 m/s is stopped by two parallel springs compressed 150 mm. C = 6, Sut = 1250, τ = 0.5Sut. Active coils per spring?

KE = ½(1500)(1.5)² = 1687.5 J. Two springs: 2 × ½P(150) = 1687.5 × 10³, so P = 11 250 N each. 625 = 1.2525 × 8(11 250)(6)/πd², so d = 18.56 → 20 mm and D = 120. 150 = 8(11 250)(120)³N/(81 370 × 20⁴), so N = 12.56 → 13. Free length = 300 + 28 + 155.29 ≈ 485 mm.

Ex. 10.7 (): eight identical parallel springs give 2400 N in total on a clutch, about 15 mm deflection each, C = 8, Sut = 1390, τ = 0.3Sut. Wire diameter required (before rounding)?

P = 2400/8 = 300 N per spring. τ = 417, K = 1.184: 417 = 1.184 × 8(300)(8)/πd², so d = 4.17 → 5 mm, D = 40, N = 4.97 → 5, Nt = 7, free length 57 mm, k = 19.87 N/mm.

Ex. 10.8 (): a 50 mm safety valve is held by a tension spring through a lever (valve at 75 mm, spring at 150 mm from the pivot). It opens when the pressure rises from 0.5 to 0.6 N/mm², with 5 mm valve lift. Required spring stiffness?

Moments: Ps(150) = Pv(75), so Ps = Pv/2. Closed: (π/4)(50)²(0.5)/2 = 490.87 N; open: 589.05 N. Spring stretch = 5 × 150/75 = 10 mm, so k = (589.05 − 490.87)/10 = 9.82 N/mm. Then d = 6.28 → 7 mm, D = 56, N = 14.16 → 15.

Ex. 10.9 (): a 50 mm safety valve blows off at 1.5 MPa. Initial compression 25 mm, maximum lift 10 mm, C = 6, Sut = 1500, τ = 0.3Sut. Wire diameter required (before rounding)?

P₁ = (π/4)(50)²(1.5) = 2945.24 N at 25 mm. P ∝ δ, so P₂ = 2945.24 × 35/25 = 4123.34 N. 450 = 1.2525 × 8(4123.34)(6)/πd², so d = 13.24 → 14 mm. D = 84, N = 5.6 → 6 (using P₁ and 25 mm), free length 165 mm.

10. GATE corner

A closely coiled helical spring of stiffness k is cut into two equal halves. The stiffness of each half is:

  1. 0.5k
  2. k
  3. 2k
  4. 4k
k = Gd⁴/8D³N and only N changes, from N to N/2. So k doubles.

Springs of 20 N/mm and 30 N/mm are connected in series. The combined stiffness is:

  1. 12 N/mm
  2. 25 N/mm
  3. 50 N/mm
  4. 10 N/mm
1/k = 1/20 + 1/30 = 5/60, so k = 12 N/mm. In series the combined spring is softer than either one.

The maximum stress in the wire of a helical compression spring is:

  1. Compressive stress spread uniformly across the wire section
  2. Torsional shear at the inside of the coil
  3. Torsional shear at the outside of the coil
  4. Bending stress at the top of each coil
The wire is in torsion. Direct shear and curvature both add on the inside fibre (Fig. 10.9).

The wire diameter of a spring is doubled, with D and N unchanged. Its stiffness becomes:

  1. 2k
  2. 4k
  3. 8k
  4. 16k
k ∝ d⁴, so 2⁴ = 16.

Exam traps

  • Deflection uses active coils N. Solid length uses total coils Nt.
  • When the force varies over a range (Ex. 10.2), size d with Pmax but find N from ΔP and Δδ.
  • Given the outside diameter, D = Do − d, not Do. Your slides' problem on p. 759 (6 mm wire, 75 mm OD) turns on exactly this.
  • Use the G the question gives. Bhandari uses 81 370 N/mm², while Khurmi-style questions on the slides use 84 or 80 kN/mm².
  • Free length = solid + gaps + δmax. Leaving out the clash gap is the most common lost mark.
