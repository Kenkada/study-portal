Spur gears: force analysis and beam strength

About 40 minutes · Bhandari §17.11–17.13, 17.17–17.18

Your win: you can resolve the tooth force into tangential and radial parts and get their directions right on a free-body diagram, including idler and planetary trains and two-stage gearboxes. You can derive the Lewis equation Sb = mbσbY and decide which of two meshing gears is weaker.
Warm-up (Lessons 26 and 42)
Torque in N·mm from kW and rpm?
Mt = 60 × 10⁶ (kW)/(2πn).
Pitch diameter from module and teeth?
d′ = mz.
Gear loads on a shaft in Lesson 26?
Pt = 2Mt/d′ and Pr = Pt tan α, resolved into vertical and horizontal planes.

1. The tooth force and its components (§17.11)

Force components (Eqs 17.7–17.10) \[ M_t = \frac{60\times10^6(\text{kW})}{2\pi n},\quad P_t = \frac{2M_t}{d'},\quad P_r = P_t\tan\alpha,\quad P_N = \frac{P_t}{\cos\alpha} \]

Assumptions: the force does not change as the contact point moves, a single pair of teeth carries the whole load, and the gears run slowly, so there is no dynamic load yet (Lesson 44 adds it).

Direction rules (). On the driving gear, Pt acts opposite to its rotation. On the driven gear, Pt acts with its rotation. Pr always points towards the centre of the gear it acts on. A gear held fixed (an internal ring) behaves as "driven" relative to the planet that drives against it.

2. Trains: idlers, planets and two-stage boxes

  • Idler (Ex. 17.2): it passes torque through without keeping any. So Pt is the same at both of its meshes (and so is Pr), and its shaft carries no torque but does carry a bearing load.
  • Planet on a carrier (Ex. 17.3): the planet gets Pt from the sun and an equal Pt from the fixed ring, in the same direction. Its pin therefore carries 2Pt, and the carrier torque is 2Pt × (arm length).
  • Two-stage box (Ex. 17.5): the intermediate gears share one shaft, so Pt′ = Pt(d₂′/d₃′). Resolve both gears' forces into the vertical and horizontal planes and take moments about one bearing.

Materials (–667): grey CI (FG 200/260/350) is cheap, quiet and wear-resistant but weak, and suits large gears. Plain carbon steel (50C8, 45C8, 50C4, 55C8) is for medium duty, and alloy steel (40Cr1, 30Ni4Cr1) for heavy duty. Case-hardened steel gives the best combination: a hard surface on a tough core. Bronze is for worm wheels because of its low friction. Non-metallic pinions (nylon, Bakelite) run quietly at light load, usually against a CI gear.

4. Derivation: the Lewis equation (§17.17)

Derive Sb = mbσbY (Eq. 17.16), and say what Y means.
  1. Treat the tooth as a cantilever of face width b with the tangential force Pt at its tip. Assume: Pr neglected, Pt spread evenly over b, no stress concentration, and one pair in contact.
  2. Draw a parabola inside the tooth from the load point. A parabolic cantilever has the same stress everywhere, so the real tooth is weakest where the parabola touches its flanks: section XX, thickness t, at distance h below the load.
  3. At XX: \(\sigma_b = \dfrac{M_by}{I} = \dfrac{(P_th)(t/2)}{bt^3/12} = \dfrac{6P_th}{bt^2}\Rightarrow P_t = b\sigma_b\dfrac{t^2}{6h}\).
  4. Multiply top and bottom by m: \(P_t = mb\sigma_b\Big(\dfrac{t^2}{6hm}\Big) = mb\sigma_bY\), where \(Y = \dfrac{t^2}{6hm}\) is the Lewis form factor. It is dimensionless and depends only on the tooth shape: the number of teeth and the tooth system.
  5. When σb reaches the permissible stress, Pt is the largest tangential force the tooth can carry without breaking. That is the beam strength: \(\boxed{S_b = mb\sigma_bY}\). The design condition is Sb ≥ Peff(fs).

5. Permissible bending stress, and which gear is weaker (§17.18)

  • Teeth on the driver and driven gears are loaded in one direction (repeated stress). Idler and planet teeth are loaded both ways (reversed). Bending fatigue governs, and Buckingham's estimate is used throughout: \(\boxed{\sigma_b = S_e \approx S_{ut}/3}\). For bronze it is 0.4Sut ().
  • m and b are the same for both gears, so the gear with the smaller σb·Y is weaker. With the same material, the pinion is always weaker, because it has fewer teeth and so a smaller Y.

6. Worked example (Bhandari Ex. 17.2, also slide Q2, p. 854)

Ex. 17.2 (): gear A (30 teeth) gets 3.5 kW at 700 rpm, clockwise, and drives idler B (60 teeth), which drives C (40 teeth). m = 5 mm, 20°. B's centre is placed so that the lines to A and C are at 90°. Find the shaft torques, the tooth forces and the reaction on the idler shaft.
  1. d′A = 150, d′B = 300, d′C = 200 mm. (Mt)A = 60 × 10⁶(3.5)/[2π(700)] = 47 746.5 N·mm.
  2. The idler keeps no torque: (Mt)B = 0. The same power reaches C at nC = 700 × 30/40, so (Mt)C = 47 746.5 × 40/30 = 63 662 N·mm.
  3. Pt = 2(47 746.5)/150 = 636.6 N and Pr = 636.6 tan 20° = 231.7 N. The idler transmits Pt unchanged, so the B–C mesh carries the same two forces.
  4. Directions: on A (driver, clockwise) Pt opposes the rotation. On C (driven) it follows the rotation. Every Pr points to its own gear's centre. On B, the two meshes' forces lie in two perpendicular planes at 45° to the vertical, each component being (Pt + Pr).
  5. \(R_B = \sqrt{2}\,(P_t + P_r) = \sqrt2\,(636.6 + 231.7) = \mathbf{1228\ N}\). The idler shaft carries no torque but has a real bending load.

7. Practice (Bhandari Examples)

Ex. 17.3 (): planetary train. Sun A has 30 teeth, 5 kW at 1440 rpm. Planet B has 60 teeth, fixed ring C has 150, m = 4 mm, 20°. Torque the arm D can deliver?

d′A = 120 and d′B = 240, so the arm length LD = (120 + 240)/2 = 180 mm. (Mt)A = 33 157.3 N·mm and Pt = 2(33 157.3)/120 = 552.6 N. The planet gets Pt from the sun and Pt from the ring, so its pin pushes the arm with 2Pt. Torque = 2(552.6)(180) = 198 943 N·mm = 198.94 N·m.

Ex. 17.4 (): concrete mixer. The drum takes 5 kW at 250 rpm through two identical planets F, whose pins are fixed to the drum, around sun E (35 teeth) inside fixed ring A (65 teeth), m = 5 mm. Tangential force between E and F?

Drum torque = 60 × 10⁶(5)/[2π(250)] = 190 986 N·mm. The pins are a = (325 + 175)/2 = 250 mm apart, and each pin carries 2(Pt)EF, so the torque on the drum = 2(Pt)EF × a. (Pt)EF = 190 986/500 = 381.97 N and (Pr)EF = 139.03 N. Between pinion B and gear C (400 mm): 2(381.97)(87.5) = (Pt)CB(200), so (Pt)CB = 334.22 N.

Ex. 17.5 (): two-stage box, pinion 1 (20 teeth) takes 10 kW at 1440 rpm, gears 2 and 4 have 50 teeth, pinion 3 has 20, a = 175 mm per stage, 20°. On shaft EF (300 mm between bearings) gear 2 sits 50 mm from E and pinion 3 sits 250 mm from E. Horizontal reaction at bearing F?

m = 5. Pt = 2(66 314.56)/100 = 1326.29 N and Pr = 482.73 N (1–2 mesh). Pt′ = 1326.29(250/100) = 3315.73 N and Pr′ = 1206.83 N (3–4 mesh). Horizontal moments about E: 3315.73(250) − 1326.29(50) = RF(300), so (RF)h = 2542.06 N. Similarly (RF)v = 1086.15, (RE)v = 603.41 and (RE)h = 552.62 N.

Ex. 17.8 (), first step: a 20-tooth Fe 410 pinion (Sut = 410) meshes with a 50-tooth FG 200 gear (Sut = 200). Which is weaker? Enter the smaller σb·Y.

Pinion: (410/3)(0.320) = 43.73. Gear: (200/3)(0.408) = 27.20. The gear is weaker and must be designed, even though it has more teeth. The material difference outweighs the larger Y.

Ex. 17.10 (), first step: 20-tooth pinion, 41-tooth gear, m = 3, b = 40 mm, both steel with Sut = 600. Beam strength?

Same material, so the pinion is weaker. σb = 600/3 = 200 and Y = 0.320. Sb = 3(40)(200)(0.32) = 7680 N.

8. GATE corner

The component of the gear tooth force that transmits power is the:

  1. radial component, towards the gear centre
  2. tangential component, along the pitch circle
  3. normal force, along the line of action
  4. axial component, along the shaft axis
Mt = Pt(d′/2). Pr only separates the gears and loads the bearings.

In the Lewis equation the form factor Y depends on:

  1. the module and the face width
  2. the material and its strength
  3. the pitch line velocity of gears
  4. the number of teeth and tooth system
Y = t²/6hm depends only on the tooth shape, which is set by z and the profile system (Table 17.3).

A pinion and gear are made of the same material. Which statement is true?

  1. The pinion is weaker, because its Y is smaller
  2. The gear is weaker, because it is much larger
  3. Both are equally strong, since materials match
  4. The weaker one depends only on face width
With σb, m and b equal, Sb ∝ Y, and Y grows with z.

Destructive pitting of gear teeth is a failure due to:

  1. abrasive particles carried in the lubricant
  2. bending fatigue at the root fillet section
  3. surface fatigue from repeated contact stress
  4. welding and tearing after film breakdown
Pitting happens when the Hertz contact stress exceeds the surface endurance strength. Scoring is the welding-and-tearing failure.

Exam traps

  • Use the torque of the gear whose diameter you divide by. Pt is the same on both teeth of a mesh, but Mt differs.
  • Directions: the driver's Pt opposes its rotation and the driven gear's follows it. Most free-body diagram marks are lost here.
  • An idler shaft has zero torque but is not unloaded. Combine the two meshes' forces vectorially.
  • For different materials, compare σbY. Do not assume the pinion is weaker.
