Unit 2 · Lesson 23

Design of screw, nut and screw jack

About 35 minutes · Bhandari §6.14–6.15

Your win: you can carry a screw jack design from load to finished parts: screw size (compression, torsion, bending, buckling), nut length from bearing pressure, thread shear, cup and collar, and handle. This is the long design question that appears almost every year.
Warm-up (Lessons 2, 21, 22)
Raising torque for a square thread?
Mt = (Wdm/2) tan(φ + α)
Bearing pressure vs crushing: which applies between screw and nut?
Bearing pressure: the surfaces slide, so the allowable value is low (about 10 N/mm²).
Principal shear stress from σ and τ?
\(\sqrt{(\sigma/2)^2 + \tau^2}\)

1. Stresses in screw and nut

Screw body (Eqs a–c, §6.14) \[ \sigma_c = \frac{W}{\frac{\pi}{4}d_c^2} \qquad \tau = \frac{16 M_t}{\pi d_c^3} \qquad \tau_{max} = \sqrt{(\sigma/2)^2 + \tau^2} \]

Use the torque that actually acts on the section: between nut and handle it is Mt (thread torque); between handle and cup it is (Mt)c. No section carries both ().

Threads and nut (Eqs 6.21–6.23) \[ \tau_s = \frac{W}{\pi d_c t z}\ \text{(screw thread root)} \qquad \tau_n = \frac{W}{\pi d t z}\ \text{(nut thread root)} \qquad S_b = \frac{4W}{\pi z (d^2 - d_c^2)} \]

t = p/2 for square threads; z = threads engaged; nut length H = z·p. Allowable Sb (Table 6.4): steel/bronze screw jack 11–17; steel/cast iron screw jack 13–17; hoisting screw 4–10; lead screw 1–1.5 N/mm² ().

Materials (, ): screw of 30C8/40C8/45C8 steel (case-hardened for lead screws); nut of bronze (softer, conformable, low friction, so the cheaper part takes the wear); frame and cup of grey cast iron FG 200 (cheap, complex shape, strong in compression); handle of 30C8.

2. Bhandari's screw jack design project (100 kN, 0.5 m lift)

Design project §6.15 (–213). W = 100 kN; lift 500 mm; manual (two workers, 0.9 × 2 × 400 = 720 N); (fs) = 5; screw 30C8 (Syt = 400, E = 207 000); bronze nut (Sb = 10 N/mm², μ up to 0.18 for poor lubrication).
  1. First size from compression: σc = 400/5 = 80 ⇒ dc = √(4 × 10⁵/(π × 80)) = 39.9 mm. Increase it to allow for torsion and bending: try Sq 60 × 9, so dc = 51, dm = 55.5 mm.
  2. Self-locking: tan α = 9/(π × 55.5), so α = 2.95°; φ = tan⁻¹ 0.18 = 10.20°. φ > α ✓.
  3. Thread torque: Mt = 10⁵ × 27.75 × tan(13.15°) = 648 316 N·mm.
  4. Stresses at section XX (just below the handle): τ = 16Mt/πdc³ = 24.89; bending from the hand force at 570 mm: σb = 32(720 × 570)/π(51)³ = 31.51. (The direct compression 48.95 is neglected, since compression closes cracks.) τmax = √(15.76² + 24.89²) = 29.46, so (fs) = 200/29.46 = 6.79 > 5 ✓.
  5. Buckling (one end fixed in the nut, one free, n = 0.25; l = 550 mm; k = dc/4 = 12.75): l/k = 43.1 < critical 50.5, so it is a short column and Johnson's equation applies: Pcr = 519 kN, (fs) = 5.19 ✓.
  6. Nut: z = 4 × 10⁵/(π × 10 × (60² − 51²)) = 12.75, so 13 threads and H = 13 × 9 = 117 mm. Nut thread shear τn = 10⁵/(π × 60 × 4.5 × 13) = 9.07 N/mm² (fs 10.5 on 0.5 Sut, bronze) ✓. Nut OD ≈ 2d.
  7. Cup and collar: sliding collar (Do = 1.6d, Di = 0.8d, μ = 0.2) adds 720 000 N·mm, which would need a 1.9 m handle. Impractical, so fit a thrust ball bearing (C = 10⁵ × 1.81/3 = 121.6 kN, giving SKF 51410), which makes collar friction negligible.
  8. Handle: 720 × lh = 648 316 ⇒ lh = 900 → 910 mm. Bending: 80 = 32(720 × 910)/πdh³ ⇒ dh = 43.7 → 45 mm.

The design also lists safety measures: a proof test at 150% load, a warning plate, and an instruction manual (). A one-paragraph mention of these earns marks in the "design a screw jack" question.

3. Practice (Bhandari Examples)

Ex. 6.8 (): C-clamp, double-start square thread, trial size Sq 22 × 5, clamping force 5 kN, μ = 0.15 threads, collar 17/6 mm with μc = 0.17, operator force 250 N. Radius of the ball handle?

l = 10, dm = 19.5, α = 9.271°. Mt = 5000 × 9.75 × tan(17.80°) = 15 654; (Mt)c = 0.17 × 5000 × 23/4 = 4887.5. Rm = 20 541/250 = 82.2 mm.

Checks: at section AA, τ = 21.29 and σb = 142.5 from the 250 N handle force at 275 mm, giving τmax = 74.4 and (fs) = 2.22 ✓. Nut (cast iron, Sb = 15): z = 2.18, so 3 threads and a 15 mm nut.

Ex. 6.9 (): screw clamp Sq 22 × 5, capacity 750 N, μ = 0.15, collar mean radius 15 mm; the handle (30C8, Syt = 400) should take a permanent set if the operator's 20 N exceeds the rated capacity. Handle diameter?

Total torque = 1714.7 + 0.15 × 750 × 15 = 3402 N·mm, so the handle length = 3402/20 = 170 mm. At yield: 400 = 32 × 3402/πd³ ⇒ d = 4.42 mm.

Ex. 6.10 (): fly-press punching 50 mm circles from 1.5 mm sheet (Sus = 375). Triple-start Sq 50 × 8 screw, bronze nut Sb = 18 N/mm². Nut length?

W = π(50)(1.5)(375) = 88 357 N. z = 4W/(π × 18 × (50² − 42²)) = 8.49, so 9 threads; nut length = 9 × 8 = 72 mm.

Screw checks: σc = 63.8, τ = 45.3, τmax = 55.4, (fs) = 3.61 ✓. Thread shear 18.6 (screw), 15.6 (nut). η = 51.2%. Ball mass: 104.85 kg for the pair, each Ø240 mm.

Ex. 6.5 (): shaft straightener, Sq 80 × 10, axial force 10 kN, nut length 40 mm. Bearing pressure on the nut threads?

z = 40/10 = 4; dc = 70. Sb = 4(10 000)/(π × 4 × (80² − 70²)) = 2.12 N/mm². (Rim force 388.9 N; efficiency 16.4%.)

In the screw jack design, why was the sliding collar replaced by a thrust ball bearing?

  1. The sliding collar failed in bearing pressure
  2. Collar friction made the handle impractically long
  3. The ball bearing makes the screw self-locking
  4. Cast-iron cups cannot carry a sliding collar
Collar torque (720 N·m) exceeded the thread torque, so the handle would have had to be 1.9 m long. A thrust ball bearing makes collar friction negligible, giving a 0.91 m handle ().

Why is the nut made of bronze and the screw of steel?

  1. Bronze is stronger in shear than steel
  2. A bronze nut makes the screw efficiency exceed 50%
  3. Bronze can be cast with square threads directly
  4. Wear goes to the softer, cheaper nut, sparing the screw
Wear is unavoidable with sliding threads and goes to the softer surface. Replacing a nut is cheaper than replacing the screw. Bronze also has low friction and good conformability ().

Exam traps

  • Adding thread torque and collar torque at the same section of the screw.
  • Forgetting the buckling check for a long lift.
  • Nut length from shear alone. Bearing pressure governs the number of threads.
