Unit 2 · Lesson 16

Welded joints: butt and fillet welds under direct load

About 25 minutes · Bhandari §8.1–8.11, 8.15–8.17

Your win: you can size butt and fillet welds for axial loads: parallel, transverse, mixed, unsymmetrical (angle sections), and fluctuating loads. This covers question bank Q23–Q25 and Q31.
Warm-up (Lessons 7, 14)
Name two situations where riveting is still preferred to welding.
Heat-treated or machined parts that welding heat would damage; aluminium alloys (poor weldability); dissimilar materials; vibration/impact; thin sheets (aircraft).
Ductile part, static load, stress raiser: use Kt?
No. Same for welds: Kf only under fluctuating load.

1. Welding: why, how, and its drawbacks

Advantages over riveting (): lighter (no straps, gussets or rivet holes), cheaper, leak-proof, faster, no holes so no stress concentration, neater, often stronger than the plates, easy to modify. Over castings: lighter, metal only where needed, lower capital cost (no foundry or patterns). Disadvantages: poor vibration damping, distortion and residual stresses (need stress relief), quality depends on welder skill, inspection costly.

Processes (): fusion (thermit, gas, electric arc) and heat-plus-pressure (forge, electric resistance spot/seam). Stress relief: preheating, normalising or annealing at 550–675 °C, peening.

2. Strength equations

Butt weld (Eqs 8.1–8.3) \[ P = \sigma_t\,t\,l\,\eta \]

Throat = plate thickness. Reinforcement is ignored. η = joint efficiency, if a code requires it.

Fillet welds (Eqs 8.4–8.10) \[ t = 0.707h \qquad \text{parallel: } P = 0.707\,h\,l\,\tau\ (\times2\ \text{for two welds} = 1.414hl\tau) \qquad \text{transverse: } P = 0.707\,h\,l\,\sigma_t \]

Add 15 mm to each weld length for starting and stopping the weld run. AWS static shear stress: 94 N/mm².

Exact analysis (–283): maximum shear is on the 45° plane for a parallel weld (\(\tau_{max} = P/0.707hl\)) and on the 67.5° plane for a transverse weld (\(\tau_{max} = 1.21P/hl\)). So a transverse weld is 1.17× stronger. Designing it with the parallel-weld shear equation is therefore safe and simpler.

Unsymmetrical section (angle) loaded through its CG (Eqs 8.18–8.20) \[ l_1 + l_2 = l = \frac{P}{0.707h\tau} \qquad l_1y_1 = l_2y_2 \]

y₁, y₂ are the distances from the angle's centroid to each weld line. The weld nearer the centroid is longer.

Fluctuating load (§8.16): use Kf on the weld (Jennings, Table 8.3)

Reinforced butt 1.2 · toe of transverse fillet 1.5 · end of parallel fillet 2.7 · T-butt with sharp corners 2.0. Then design with Lesson 6's \(S_e = K_aK_bK_cK_dS'_e\).

3. Worked example (Bhandari Ex. 8.5, question bank Q24)

Example 8.5 (). A plate 100 mm wide and 10 mm thick is joined by one transverse and two parallel fillet welds (h = 10). The joint must be as strong as the plate. Permissible σt = 70, τ = 50 N/mm². Find each parallel weld length.
  1. Plate strength: P = 100 × 10 × 70 = 70 000 N.
  2. Transverse weld (across the 100 mm width): P₁ = 0.707(10)(100)(70) = 49 490 N.
  3. Two parallel welds: P₂ = 1.414(10)(l)(50) = 707 l.
  4. 70 000 = 49 490 + 707 l ⇒ l = 29.0 mm; add 15 mm, so l = 44 → 45 mm.

4. Practice

Ex. 8.2: a 100 × 10 mm plate is welded by two parallel fillet welds (h = 10 mm) and carries 50 kN static. τ = 94 N/mm². Required length of each weld, including the 15 mm allowance?

50 000 = 1.414(10)(l)(94) ⇒ l = 37.6; +15 = 52.6, so 55 mm.

Question bank Q31 / Bhandari Problem 8.2: a plate 80 mm wide and 10 mm thick, one transverse and two parallel fillet welds, joint as strong as the plate. σt = 100, τ = 70 N/mm². Length of each parallel weld (book answer excludes the 15 mm)?

Plate: 80 000 N. Transverse: 0.707(10)(80)(100) = 56 560. Parallel: 1.414(10)(70)l = 989.8l. So l = 23.68 mm.

Question bank Q25 (): a 200 × 150 × 10 angle is welded along its 200 mm leg by top and bottom fillet welds (h = 10) and carries 200 kN through its centroid. τ = 75 N/mm². The centroid is 60.9 mm from the bottom (outer face of the 150 mm leg). Find the bottom weld length.

Total l = 200 000/(0.707 × 10 × 75) = 377.2 mm. Moments about the centroid: ltop(200 − 60.9) = lbot(60.9).

lbot = 377.2 × 139.1/200 = 262.4 mm; ltop = 114.8 mm. (Centroid: [200·10·100 + 140·10·5]/3400 = 60.9 mm.)

Question bank Q23 (): plate 75 × 12.5 mm, one transverse and two parallel fillet welds (h = 12.5). σt = 70, τ = 56 MPa. For fatigue loading, reduce by Kf = 1.5 (transverse) and 2.7 (parallel). Length of each parallel weld, adding 12.5 mm for start/stop (the slides' Khurmi convention)?

Plate: 75 × 12.5 × 70 = 65 625 N. Transverse: 0.707(12.5)(75)(70/1.5) = 30 932. Parallel: 1.414(12.5)(56/2.7)l = 366.6l.

l = 94.6 + 12.5 = 107.1 mm. For static loading it would be only 19.4 + 12.5 = 31.9 mm. Fatigue more than triples the weld.

Ex. 8.17: a reinforced butt weld (throat 25 mm) carries ±100 kN. Weld metal Sut = 450; Ka = 0.52 (forged-equivalent), Kb = 0.85, 90% reliability, Kf = 1.2, (fs) = 2. Find the weld length.

Se = 0.52(0.85)(0.897)(1/1.2)(225) = 74.34; allowable amplitude 37.17. l = 100 000/(25 × 37.17) = 107.6 → 110 mm.

Ex. 8.1: a gas tank (D = 2.5 m, t = 12 mm) has hemispherical ends butt-welded on (σt = 85, η = 0.85). Allowable internal pressure?

Weld force = σt t (πD) η = 85(12)(π × 2500)(0.85) = 6.81 × 10⁶ N. Pressure = force/(πD²/4) = 1.39 N/mm².

Why is a single transverse fillet weld not preferred?

  1. It cannot carry any tensile load
  2. It needs a bigger leg than a parallel weld
  3. The unwelded plate edge can warp out of shape
  4. It always fails before the plate does
With only one transverse weld, the free edge of the other plate can deflect, so a double transverse weld is preferred ().

Why is a normal (flat-faced, triangular) fillet weld preferred over a convex weld?

  1. The convex weld has a smaller throat
  2. The convex weld costs more and concentrates stress
  3. The normal weld can be laid without filler metal
  4. Codes prohibit convex welds on machine parts
A convex weld needs more filler metal and labour and produces more stress concentration ().

Exam traps

  • Using h instead of 0.707h in the throat area.
  • Forgetting the start/stop allowance: 15 mm in Bhandari (some books use 12.5 mm). Say which.
  • Applying Kf to static loads. Only fluctuating loads need it.
