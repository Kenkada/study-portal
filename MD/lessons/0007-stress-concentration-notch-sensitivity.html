1. What Kt is

The elementary formulas (P/A, My/I, Tr/J) assume a uniform section. Holes, shoulders, keyways, threads and scratches make the stress shoot up locally ():

Theoretical stress concentration factor (Eq. 5.1) \[ K_t = \frac{\sigma_{max}}{\sigma_o} = \frac{\tau_{max}}{\tau_o} \]

\(\sigma_o\) is the nominal stress at the minimum section: for a plate with a hole, \(\sigma_o = P/(w-d)t\); for a shouldered shaft in bending, \(\sigma_o = 32M_b/\pi d^3\) with d the smaller diameter.

Kt depends only on geometry and comes from Peterson's charts (Bhandari Figs 5.2–5.6). Elliptical hole: \(K_t = 1 + 2a/b\), where a is the half-width across the load. A circular hole gives Kt = 3. A sharp crack (b → 0) gives Kt → ∞ ().

Causes (): variation in material properties (blow holes, inclusions); load applied over a small area (gear teeth, cam and follower, ball and race); abrupt change of section (shoulders); discontinuities (oil holes, keyways, threads); machining scratches and stamp marks.

2. When to use it: the rule examiners love

Material and loadUse stress concentration?Why ()
Ductile, staticNo, ignore KtLocal yielding redistributes the stress, and the yielded zone is tiny.
Ductile, fluctuatingYes, use Kf (via Kd = 1/Kf)The local peak can exceed the endurance limit and start a fatigue crack.
Brittle (cast iron), static or fluctuatingYes, use KtNo yielding, so the peak stress cracks the material directly.

3. Notch sensitivity: Kt overstates the real damage

Real materials lose less endurance limit than Kt predicts. The fatigue stress concentration factor Kf is the endurance limit of a notch-free specimen divided by that of the notched one. The notch sensitivity q links the two (–155):

Eqs 5.11, 5.12, 5.27 \[ q = \frac{K_f - 1}{K_t - 1} \qquad K_f = 1 + q\,(K_t - 1) \qquad K_d = \frac{1}{K_f} \]

q = 0: no sensitivity, Kf = 1. q = 1: fully sensitive, Kf = Kt. q comes from Figs 5.22–5.23 (it rises with Sut and notch radius). If in doubt, take q = 1 (safe).

4. Reducing stress concentration

Think of stress as fluid flowing through the part. Sharp bends in the "force-flow lines" crowd them together. Smooth the bends (–148):

  • Tension member with a notch: add multiple notches, drill extra holes, or remove unneeded material (the principle of minimisation of material).
  • Shouldered shaft: use the largest fillet radius the mating part allows, undercut the shoulder, or cut a relief notch.
  • Keyway: fillet the bottom corners or drill two holes beside it. Use Kt = 3 for a keyed shaft under combined bending and torsion.
  • Threaded rod: undercut between shank and thread, or reduce the shank to the core diameter (straight force flow, no concentration).

5. Worked example (Bhandari Ex. 5.1, question bank Q13)

Example 5.1 (). A flat plate of grey cast iron FG 200, (fs) = 2.5, carries a static 5 kN tensile force. It is 45 mm wide, stepping down to 30 mm through a 5 mm fillet, and the 30 mm part has a 15 mm hole. Kt = 1.8 at the fillet and 2.16 at the hole. Find the thickness t.
  1. Brittle material, so use Kt even though the load is static. Allowable \(\sigma_{max} = 200/2.5 = 80\) N/mm².
  2. Fillet section (width 30): \(\sigma_o = 5000/30t\), so \(\sigma_{max} = 1.8 \times 5000/30t = 300/t\).
  3. Hole section (net width 30 − 15 = 15): \(\sigma_o = 5000/15t\), so \(\sigma_{max} = 2.16 \times 5000/15t = 720/t\).
  4. The hole governs: \(720/t = 80 \Rightarrow t = 9\) mm.

6. Practice

Question bank Q14 (Bhandari Ex. 5.3, ): a plate of hot-rolled steel 20C8 (Sut = 440 N/mm²), 50 mm wide with a 10 mm central hole, carries a completely reversed axial load of ±30 kN. q = 0.8, Kt = 2.51, Ka = 0.67, Kb = 0.85, reliability 90%, (fs) = 2. Find t for infinite life.

\(K_f = 1 + 0.8(2.51 - 1) = 2.208\), so \(K_d = 0.4529\).

\(S_e = 0.67(0.85)(0.897)(0.4529)(220) = 50.9\) N/mm². Axial: \((S_e)_a = 0.8(50.9) = 40.72\). Permissible amplitude \(= 20.36\) N/mm².

\(\dfrac{30\,000}{(50-10)t} = 20.36\Rightarrow t = 36.84\) mm.

Bhandari Ex. 5.2: a non-rotating shaft of brittle material (Sut = 300 N/mm², (fs) = 3) carries 2.5 kN at mid-span between bearings 1000 mm apart. The central part has diameter 1.1d. It steps down to d through a fillet 350 mm from each bearing, where Kt = 1.61. Find d.

Reactions 1250 N. Fillet: \(M = 1250\times350\), \(\sigma = 1.61\times\dfrac{32(437\,500)}{\pi d^3} = \dfrac{7.175\times10^6}{d^3}\).

Centre: \(M = 1250\times500\), \(\sigma = \dfrac{32(625\,000)}{\pi(1.1d)^3} = \dfrac{4.783\times10^6}{d^3}\). The fillet governs.

\(7.175\times10^6/d^3 = 100\Rightarrow d = 41.55\) mm. Brittle material, so Kt is used under static load.

A mild-steel bracket with a drilled hole carries a steady static load. How do you treat the hole?

  1. Multiply the nominal stress by Kt = 3
  2. Multiply the nominal stress by Kf from q
  3. Ignore Kt; use the net section only
  4. Reduce Syt by the factor Kd
Ductile material under static load: local yielding at the hole redistributes the stress, so Kt is ignored (). You still use the reduced net area for the nominal stress.

If q = 0 for a material, what is Kf?

  1. 1, the notch has no fatigue effect
  2. Kt, the full theoretical value
  3. 0, the endurance limit vanishes
  4. 2, the usual design assumption
\(K_f = 1 + q(K_t-1)\). With q = 0, Kf = 1: the material is insensitive to the notch.

Which change reduces the stress concentration at a shaft shoulder?

  1. Making the fillet radius smaller
  2. Undercutting to allow a larger fillet
  3. Increasing the shoulder step height
  4. Adding a sharp groove beside it
A larger fillet radius smooths the force flow. Undercutting the shoulder lets you fit a larger radius without changing the seat for the mating part (Bhandari Fig. 5.10).

Exam traps

  • Using Kt for a ductile part under static load. Most marks are lost here.
  • Multiplying the stress by Kf and dividing Se by Kf. That counts it twice. Bhandari reduces Se through Kd.
  • Using the gross width instead of the net width (w − d) at a hole.
  • Checking only one section. Check every candidate (fillet, hole, mid-span) and keep the worst.
