Shafts: design on strength and torsional rigidity

About 30 minutes · Bhandari §9.1–9.3, 9.5–9.7

Your win: you can derive the equivalent torque and equivalent bending moment, size solid and hollow shafts on strength and on twist, and prove why a hollow shaft is stronger than a solid one of the same weight. Each of these comes up as a derivation question and as a GATE-style one-liner.

1. Shafts and where they're used

A transmission shaft is a rotating element of circular section that carries gears, pulleys or sprockets and transmits power. It is stepped (largest in the middle, smallest at the bearing seats), and the shoulders locate the parts. Fillets reduce stress concentration ().

NameMeaningApplication
Axlecarries wheels or drums; mostly bending, little or no torquerailway wagon axle (automobile rear axle also carries torque)
Spindleshort rotating shaftmachine-tool spindles, lathe drive
Countershaft / jackshaftsecondary shaft driven from the main shaftmulti-stage gearboxes
Line shaftshafts coupled end to end, driving many machines by beltsold group-drive workshops (obsolete)
Flexible shaftwire-coil core in a casing: low bending stiffness, high torsional stiffnessspeedometer drives, portable grinders, concrete vibrators

Materials: 30C8, 40C8 (machinery steels); 45C8, 50C8 or alloy steels (40Cr4Mo2, 40Ni14…) for higher strength. Hot-rolled, then turned and ground. Cold-drawn shafts are stronger but distort when keyways are cut, because residual stresses are released ().

2. Derivation: equivalent torque and equivalent bending moment

Derive the design equations for a solid shaft under bending moment Mb and torque Mt (Bhandari Eqs 9.6–9.10).
  1. At the outer fibre: \(\sigma_x = \dfrac{32M_b}{\pi d^3}\) (bending) and \(\tau = \dfrac{16M_t}{\pi d^3}\) (torsion). There is no σy.
  2. From Mohr's circle (centre σx/2, radius √((σx/2)² + τ²)): \(\sigma_1 = \dfrac{\sigma_x}{2} + \sqrt{\left(\dfrac{\sigma_x}{2}\right)^2 + \tau^2}\) and \(\tau_{max} = \sqrt{\left(\dfrac{\sigma_x}{2}\right)^2 + \tau^2}\).
  3. Substitute: \(\dfrac{\sigma_x}{2} = \dfrac{16M_b}{\pi d^3}\), so \(\tau_{max} = \dfrac{16}{\pi d^3}\sqrt{M_b^2 + M_t^2}\).
  4. Compare with pure torsion, τ = 16T/πd³. The shaft behaves as if under a single torque \(\boxed{T_e = \sqrt{M_b^2 + M_t^2}}\), the equivalent torsional moment. It goes with maximum shear stress theory (ductile shafts).
  5. Likewise \(\sigma_1 = \dfrac{16}{\pi d^3}\left[M_b + \sqrt{M_b^2+M_t^2}\right] = \dfrac{32}{\pi d^3}\cdot\tfrac12\left[M_b + \sqrt{M_b^2+M_t^2}\right]\). Compare with pure bending, σ = 32M/πd³: \(\boxed{M_e = \tfrac12\left[M_b + \sqrt{M_b^2+M_t^2}\right]}\), the equivalent bending moment. It goes with maximum principal stress theory.

Bhandari writes the principal-stress result as \(\sigma_1 = \frac{16}{\pi d^3}[M_b + \sqrt{M_b^2+M_t^2}]\) and calls the bracket the "equivalent bending moment". Many GATE sources define Me with the ½ so that it sits in 32Me/πd³. Both are the same physics. Know which form the question uses.

Design equations (Eqs 9.8–9.13) \[ \frac{16}{\pi d^3}\sqrt{M_b^2+M_t^2} = \frac{0.5S_{yt}}{(fs)}\ \text{(MSST)} \qquad \frac{16}{\pi d^3}\left[M_b+\sqrt{M_b^2+M_t^2}\right] = \frac{S_{yt}}{(fs)}\ \text{(MPST)} \] \[ \theta^\circ = \frac{584\,M_t\,l}{G\,d^4}\quad\text{(limits: 0.25°/m machine tools; 3°/m line shafts; G ≈ 79 300 N/mm²)} \]

3. Derivation: why a hollow shaft is stronger for the same weight

Derive the torque-capacity ratio of a hollow shaft (k = di/do) to a solid shaft of the same material, length and weight.
  1. Same weight means the same area: \(d_o^2(1-k^2) = d^2\), so \(d_o = d/\sqrt{1-k^2}\).
  2. Torque capacity at the same τ: solid \(T_s = \frac{\pi}{16}d^3\tau\); hollow \(T_h = \frac{\pi}{16}d_o^3(1-k^4)\tau\).
  3. \(\dfrac{T_h}{T_s} = \dfrac{d_o^3(1-k^4)}{d^3} = \dfrac{1-k^4}{(1-k^2)^{3/2}} = \boxed{\dfrac{1+k^2}{\sqrt{1-k^2}}}\).
  4. For k = 0.6: (1.36)/0.8 = 1.70. The hollow shaft carries 70% more torque for the same weight, because the material near the centre (where stress is nearly zero) has been moved outward where it works hardest ().
Hollow shaft (Eqs 9.18–9.26): replace d³ by do³(1 − k⁴) and d⁴ by do⁴(1 − k⁴) \[ \tau_{max} = \frac{16\sqrt{M_b^2+M_t^2}}{\pi d_o^3(1-k^4)} \qquad \theta^\circ = \frac{584\,M_tl}{G\,d_o^4(1-k^4)} \]

Hollow shafts: stiffer, stronger and with higher natural frequency per unit weight, but costlier and bigger in diameter. Applications: automobile propeller shafts, railway axles, drill rods (coolant passage), epicyclic gearboxes with one shaft running inside another ().

4. Practice (Bhandari Examples)

Ex. 9.2 (): 7.5 kW at 360 rpm; maximum bending moment 952 927.5 N·mm at bearing A (from overhung pulley loads); 40C8 (Syt = 380), (fs) = 3. Diameter on strength (MSST)?

Mt = 60×10⁶ × 7.5/(2π × 360) = 198 944 N·mm; τ = 0.5(380)/3 = 63.33. \(d^3 = \frac{16}{\pi(63.33)}\sqrt{952\,927.5^2 + 198\,944^2}\Rightarrow d = 42.78\) mm.

Same shaft: diameter for a twist of 0.5° per metre (G = 79 300)?

\(d^4 = \frac{584(198\,944)(1000)}{79\,300(0.5)}\Rightarrow d = 41.37\) mm. Strength governs here (42.78 mm).

Ex. 9.9 (): Mt = 144 000 N·mm acts over 800 mm between two gears; allowed twist 0.25°/m (so 0.2° over 800 mm); G = 79 300. Diameter on rigidity? (Strength gave only 32.95 mm.)

\(d^4 = 584(144\,000)(800)/(79\,300 \times 0.2)\Rightarrow d = 45.38\) mm. Rigidity governs, which is why you check both (, Step III).

Ex. 9.10 (): hollow propeller shaft, 45 kW at 500 rpm, di = 0.6do, τ = 84 N/mm². Outer diameter?

Mt = 859 437 N·mm. \(84 = \frac{16(859\,437)}{\pi d_o^3(1-0.6^4)}\Rightarrow d_o = 39.12\), di = 23.47 mm.

Ex. 9.11 (): hollow shaft (k = 0.6), 40C8, (fs) = 3. A 1000 mm pulley overhangs the bearing by 250 mm; belt tensions 3 kN and 1 kN (vertical); pulley weight 500 N. Outer diameter?

Mt = (3000 − 1000)(500) = 10⁶; Mb = (3000 + 1000 + 500)(250) = 1.125×10⁶ N·mm.

\(63.33 = \frac{16\sqrt{1.125^2+1^2}\times10^6}{\pi d_o^3(1-0.1296)}\Rightarrow d_o = 51.81\), di = 31.09 mm.

Ex. 9.12 (, a derivation): a solid shaft (diameter d) is replaced by a hollow shaft equally strong in torsion and half the weight per metre. Find do/d.

Equal strength: \((d_o^4 - d_i^4)/d_o = d^3\). Half weight: \(d_o^2 - d_i^2 = d^2/2\), so \(d_i^4 = (d_o^2 - d^2/2)^2\).

Eliminate di: \(d_o^4 - d^3d_o = d_o^4 - d_o^2d^2 + d^4/4\Rightarrow d_o^2 - d\,d_o - d^2/4 = 0\Rightarrow d_o = \frac{1+\sqrt2}{2}d = 1.207d\).

5. GATE corner (GATE-style conceptual questions)

A ductile shaft carries M = 3 kN·m and T = 4 kN·m. By maximum shear stress theory, the equivalent torque is:

  1. 7.0 kN·m, the sum of the two
  2. 5.0 kN·m, the root of the sum of squares
  3. 4.0 kN·m, the larger of the two
  4. 4.0 kN·m, half of M plus T
Te = √(3² + 4²) = 5 kN·m. (The equivalent bending moment with the ½ would be ½(3 + 5) = 4 kN·m. Don't confuse them.)

At a fixed allowable twist per metre, doubling the torque changes the required shaft diameter by a factor of:

  1. 2
  2. 21/3 ≈ 1.26
  3. √2 ≈ 1.41
  4. 21/4 ≈ 1.19
θ ∝ T/d⁴, so d ∝ T1/4. On strength d ∝ T1/3. These exponents are favourite GATE ratios.

A hollow shaft has the same outer diameter as a solid one and di/do = 0.5. Its torque capacity at the same stress is:

  1. 93.75% of the solid shaft's
  2. 75.00% of the solid shaft's
  3. 50.00% of the solid shaft's
  4. 87.50% of the solid shaft's
Th/Ts = 1 − k⁴ = 1 − 0.0625 = 0.9375, while the weight drops to 1 − k² = 75%. Removing the core costs little strength.

A flexible shaft must have:

  1. High bending stiffness and low torsional stiffness
  2. Low bending stiffness and low torsional stiffness
  3. Low bending stiffness and high torsional stiffness
  4. High bending stiffness and high torsional stiffness
It must bend round obstacles yet transmit torque ().

Exam traps

  • Using the bending moment at the pulley when the maximum is at a bearing (overhung pulleys). Always draw the BMD.
  • Forgetting to combine vertical and horizontal moments: \(M = \sqrt{M_V^2 + M_H^2}\) at each section.
  • Checking strength only when a twist limit is given.
