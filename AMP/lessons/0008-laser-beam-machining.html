Laser beam machining: light focused hot enough to boil diamond

A laser's light is one colour, in step and barely spreading, so a simple lens can squeeze it into a spot with enormous power density. That spot melts and vaporises almost anything.

Producing a laser

LASER = Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation.

  1. Absorption. An atom absorbs a photon of the right frequency and jumps from a lower energy level q to a higher level p.
  2. Spontaneous emission. An excited atom drops back on its own, emitting a photon in a random direction and phase.
  3. Stimulated emission. A passing photon of the right frequency triggers an excited atom to emit a clone photon: same wavelength, phase, direction and energy. This is the basis of the laser.
  4. Population inversion. Normally more atoms sit in the lower level, so absorption wins. A strong pumping source (flash lamp, electric discharge) pushes most atoms into the upper level. Now stimulated emission dominates and light is amplified.
  5. Feedback (resonator). A fully reflecting mirror at one end and a partially reflecting mirror at the other bounce photons back and forth through the medium. Each pass stimulates more emission. A small fraction leaks out of the partial mirror as the beam.

Pumping methods: optical (flash lamp, ruby and Nd:YAG), direct electron excitation in a gas discharge (argon, CO₂), and atom–atom collision (He–Ne) (Jagadeesha §8.2).

Why laser light focuses so well

It is monochromatic (no colour-correction needed in the lens), coherent (waves in phase) and has low divergence. So a simple lens gives a tiny spot. Focused spot diameter ≈ focal length × divergence angle: \(W = f\theta\) (Jain, p.197).

Working principle of LBM

  1. The beam is focused to a spot near the work surface. For machining the power density must be about 1.5 × 10⁶ to 1.5 × 10⁸ W/cm²; welding needs only 1.5 × 10⁴ to 1.5 × 10⁵ W/cm² (Jain, p.190).
  2. Part of the light is reflected, part absorbed, depending on the material's reflectivity and absorptivity at that wavelength.
  3. Absorbed energy heats a tiny volume past melting and boiling. Laser machining is a high-speed ablation: vapour forms so fast that its recoil pressure blasts the remaining melt out as droplets (Jagadeesha §8.4.1).
  4. A coaxial assist gas clears melt from the cut and keeps debris off the lens. O₂ adds exothermic oxidation and cuts steel faster but leaves an oxidised edge and larger HAZ; Ar or N₂ give clean edges for parts to be welded later (Jain, p.198).

Process parameters

  • Power / pulse energy and pulse duration. High peak power (high energy in a short pulse) and high power density give good holes. Drilling time falls as energy per pulse rises. Pulse durations of 0.1–2.5 ms suit deep holes (Jain, p.194).
  • Repetition rate. Many low-energy, high-frequency pulses give better hole quality than one high-energy pulse.
  • Wavelength vs material absorptivity. Shorter wavelength is absorbed better by most metals.
  • Focal length, divergence, focal position. Low divergence and long focal length for deep holes; focus just below the surface for straight-sided holes; larger f gives a larger depth of focus.
  • Assist gas type and pressure; traverse speed. Faster feed gives a thinner HAZ.
  • Work properties. Reflectivity, thermal conductivity, melting and boiling points.

Capabilities

  • Drilling (percussion): holes about 0.125–1.25 mm, depth-to-diameter up to 100:1 (Jain); taper, recast and HAZ (0.0025–0.1 mm). Repeatability about ±0.025 mm or ±10% of diameter. Holes larger than ~1.2 mm are trepanned (beam traces the outline) or contour-cut.
  • Cutting: omnidirectional, pierces anywhere, no force. An ~800 W laser cuts most metal plates up to ~3 mm.
  • Marking: ns pulses scanned by computer, up to 30 characters per second, grooves up to 0.25 mm deep.
  • Overall machining efficiency is very low (Jain: usually below 1%).

Applications, advantages, limitations

  • Cooling holes in superalloy turbine blades and vanes; the most common small-hole drilling process for them (Jain, p.196).
  • Holes in diamond wire-drawing dies; sapphire and ruby watch bearings.
  • Cutting sheet metal, plastics, composites, ceramics; scribing silicon wafers; resistor trimming; marking; controlled fracture of brittle parts; micromachining.

Advantages

  • No contact, no force, no tool wear
  • Any material, conductive or not, including diamond, ceramics, refractory metals
  • Works through any transparent medium; reaches awkward spots
  • Very small holes; easy CNC control of beam
  • Small HAZ for short pulses

Limitations

  • Very low efficiency; high capital and running cost
  • Highly reflective, conductive metals (Al, Cu) are hard to machine
  • Tapered, not perfectly round holes; recast and HAZ; fatigue strength ↓
  • Practically limited to thin sections
  • Flash lamp life; strict eye-safety procedures

Practise

2 marksTutorial Sheet I, Q8: Why is LBM less suitable for drilling deep holes in reflective metals like aluminium or copper?

  • Al and Cu reflect most of the incident laser radiation (especially CO₂'s 10.6 µm), so little energy is absorbed.
  • Their high thermal conductivity carries absorbed heat away from the spot before it can reach melting and boiling, so efficiency collapses as the hole deepens.
  • Jain notes this is why workholding tables for LBM are made of aluminium: the beam does not damage them.
  • Better choices: EDM or EBM for deep holes in these metals.

Tutorial Q11. Required MRR = 100 mm³/s in aluminium (ρ = 2700 kg/m³). Energy per kg = 6.0 × 10⁶ J/kg; process efficiency 15%. What laser power is needed?

\[ \dot m = 100\times10^{-9} \times 2700 = 2.7\times10^{-4}\ \text{kg/s} \] \[ P_{abs} = 2.7\times10^{-4} \times 6.0\times10^{6} = 1620 \text{ W}, \qquad P = \frac{1620}{0.15} = 10{,}800 \text{ W} = 10.8 \text{ kW} \]

Tutorial Q12. A continuous laser of 500 W is focused to a spot of 0.20 mm diameter. Find the intensity.

\[ A = \frac{\pi}{4}(0.020\ \text{cm})^2 = 3.14\times10^{-4}\ \text{cm}^2, \qquad I = \frac{500}{3.14\times10^{-4}} = 1.59\times10^{6}\ \text{W/cm}^2 \]

That is right at the lower edge of the machining range in Jain.

Tutorial Q13. Thermal diffusivity α = 1 × 10⁻⁵ m²/s, pulse duration 1 ms. Thermal penetration ≈ √(αt). Express in microns.

\[ \delta = \sqrt{10^{-5}\times10^{-3}} = \sqrt{10^{-8}} = 10^{-4}\ \text{m} = 100\ \mu\text{m} \]

Shorter pulses → thinner heated layer → smaller HAZ. That is why marking uses ns pulses.

Tutorial Q14. Drill a 2.0 mm diameter through-hole in 5.0 mm thickness. Ablation removal rate = 0.01 mm³/s. How many minutes?

\[ V = \frac{\pi}{4}(2)^2(5) = 15.71 \text{ mm}^3, \qquad t = \frac{15.71}{0.01} = 1571 \text{ s} = 26.2 \text{ min} \]

A real 2 mm hole would be trepanned, removing only an annulus, which is much faster.

Tutorial Q15. Average power 200 W at 10 kHz repetition rate. Find the pulse energy.

\[ E_p = \frac{P_{avg}}{f} = \frac{200}{10{,}000} = 0.02 \text{ J} = 20 \text{ mJ} \]

Jagadeesha §8.4 example. A pulse of 20 J lasts 1 ms; beam divergence 0.002 rad; focusing lens f = 25 mm. Find the peak power density at the focal spot.

\[ P_{peak} = \frac{20}{10^{-3}} = 20 \text{ kW}, \qquad W = f\theta = 25 \times 0.002 = 0.05 \text{ mm} \] \[ A = \frac{\pi}{4}(0.005\ \text{cm})^2 = 1.96\times10^{-5}\ \text{cm}^2, \qquad I = \frac{2\times10^4}{1.96\times10^{-5}} \approx 1.0\times10^{9}\ \text{W/cm}^2 \]

Enough to vaporise any known material, diamond included.

Quick check

1. Population inversion means…

  1. More atoms in the ground state
  2. Photons travelling in the reverse direction
  3. Mirrors swapped at both tube ends
  4. More atoms in the upper level

Stimulated emission only outpaces absorption when the upper level holds more atoms than the lower one. Pumping creates this.

2. Which assist gas gives the fastest cut in mild steel?

  1. Argon
  2. Oxygen
  3. Nitrogen
  4. Helium

Oxygen burns the steel exothermically, adding heat. The price: an oxidised edge and wider HAZ. Argon gives the cleanest edge.

3. A hole larger than about 1.2 mm is usually made by…

  1. Trepanning the outline with beam
  2. One single high-energy percussion pulse
  3. Defocusing the beam far wider
  4. Switching to a ruby laser

The focused spot lacks power density for large holes, so the beam traces the outline and the slug drops out (Jain, p.198).

4. Why can LBM work an aluminium fixture table without damaging it?

  1. Aluminium has a high melting point
  2. The beam is switched off there
  3. Aluminium reflects and conducts heat well
  4. Aluminium is coated with a ceramic

Al reflects most of the beam and conducts heat away fast, so it barely heats. The same property makes Al hard to laser machine.
