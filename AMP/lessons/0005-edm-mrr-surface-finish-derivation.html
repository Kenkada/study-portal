From one spark to MRR and surface finish

Energy per spark times sparks per second gives MRR. Crater size gives roughness. Two short derivations, both built on Lesson 4.

DerivationJain p.145–147Jain Review Q9: "Derive the relationship for surface roughness"
Your win today

Derive MRR for the RC circuit and show that MRR ∝ 1/Rc; explain critical resistance; derive the roughness relation; and use them in numericals.

Part A · MRR in the RC circuit

  1. Rearrange (7.6) to get the charging time to reach \(V_b\): \[ t_c = R_cC\,\ln\!\left(\frac{1}{1 - V_b/V_0}\right) \]
  2. Discharge time is negligible, so spark frequency is \[ f_c = \frac{1}{t_c} = \frac{1}{R_cC\,\ln\!\left(\dfrac{1}{1-V_b/V_0}\right)} \]
  3. MRR is proportional to energy delivered per second: \[ \text{MRR} \propto \tfrac12 C V_b^2 \, f_c \]
  4. Substitute, with constant \(K_4\):
    \[ \text{MRR} = K_4\, C V_b^2 \cdot \frac{1}{R_cC\,\ln\!\left(\dfrac{1}{1 - V_b/V_0}\right)} = \frac{K_4\,V_b^2}{R_c\,\ln\!\left(\dfrac{1}{1 - V_b/V_0}\right)} \]

Two readings follow:

  • MRR ∝ 1/Rc. Lower the charging resistance and the capacitor recharges faster, so sparks come more often.
  • C cancels. A bigger capacitor stores more energy per spark but recharges more slowly. In this ideal model MRR does not depend on C. In practice a bigger C means bigger craters and a rougher surface, which is why C is the "finish" knob on RC machines.

Critical resistance

You cannot reduce Rc forever. Below a minimum value the capacitor never "relaxes": current keeps flowing, the gap cannot deionise, and a continuous arc forms instead of discrete sparks. Arcs damage both tool and work. That minimum is the critical resistance (Jain, p.145–146):

\[ R_{min} = \sqrt{\frac{L}{C}} \;\;\text{(theory, Bhattacharyya)}, \qquad R_{min} \ge 30\sqrt{\frac{L}{C}} \;\;\text{(experiments)} \]

Here \(L\) is the inductance of the discharge circuit.

Part B · Surface finish

  1. Each spark leaves a roughly spherical crater of depth \(h\). The CLA roughness \(H\) grows with \(h\) and falls with sparking frequency (more, smaller craters per unit time): \[ H \propto \frac{h}{f_c} \]
  2. Crater volume ∝ \(h^3\) and is proportional to spark energy: \[ h^3 \propto \tfrac12 C V_b^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad h \propto C^{1/3} V_b^{2/3} \]
  3. So \[ H = \frac{K_5\, V_b^{2/3} C^{1/3}}{f_c} \]
  4. For an RC circuit \(f_c \propto R^{-m} C^{-n}\), giving \[ H = K_6\, V_b^{2/3}\, C^{\,n + 1/3}\, R^{m} \]
  5. Experiments fit better to
    \[ H = K_7\, V_b^{0.5}\, C^{0.31\text{–}0.36} \]
What to conclude in words

To improve surface finish: reduce capacitance (less energy per spark), reduce breakdown voltage, and raise sparking frequency. Every one of these also lowers MRR. That is why EDM is done in two stages: roughing at high energy, finishing at low energy (Jain, p.130).

Numericals

Jain Problem 1. EDM on a metallic sheet with C = 15 µF, Vb = 130 V and K = 4.0. Find the surface finish using the experimental relation.

\[ H = K V_b^{0.5} C^{0.33} = 4.0 \times 130^{0.5} \times (15\times10^{-6})^{0.33} \] \[ = 4.0 \times 11.40 \times 0.02559 = 1.17\ \mu\text{m} \]

Use C in farads, as Jain does.

Jain Self-Test 2(ii). While calculating MRR, a supply voltage of 60 V was used instead of the actual 40 V. The circuit is set for maximum power delivery. Find (actual MRR) ÷ (calculated MRR).

At maximum power, \(V_b = 0.72\,V_0\), so \(\ln(1/(1-V_b/V_0))\) is the same constant in both cases. Then MRR ∝ \(V_b^2\) ∝ \(V_0^2\):

\[ \frac{\text{MRR}_{actual}}{\text{MRR}_{calc}} = \left(\frac{40}{60}\right)^2 = 0.444 \]

Answer (b) 0.44.

Jain Review Q14. A through hole of 10 mm diameter is to be drilled in a 5 mm thick plate by EDM. R = 50 Ω, Vs = 200 V, Vb = 150 V, and K = 0.18 gives MRR in mm³/min. Estimate the machining time.

\[ \text{MRR} = \frac{K V_b^2}{R\,\ln\!\left(\frac{1}{1 - V_b/V_s}\right)} = \frac{0.18 \times 150^2}{50 \times \ln 4} = \frac{4050}{69.31} = 58.4 \text{ mm}^3/\text{min} \] \[ V = \frac{\pi}{4}(10)^2(5) = 392.7 \text{ mm}^3, \qquad t = \frac{392.7}{58.4} = 6.72 \text{ min} \]

The capacitance given in the question cancels out, exactly as shown in Part A. Ignoring overcut and tool wear.

Jain Self-Test (xvii)–(xviii). (a) The capacitor's charged voltage is raised from V1 to V2 = 4V1; what happens to Ra? (b) Instead, frequency is halved; what happens to Ra?

(a) With the experimental relation \(H \propto V^{0.5}\): \(H_2 = \sqrt4\,H_1 = 2R_{a1}\). With the theoretical \(V^{2/3}\): \(4^{2/3} = 2.52 \approx 2.5\,R_{a1}\). Jain offers both 2Ra1 and 2.5Ra1 as options, so state which relation you use. The experimental one gives 2Ra1.

(b) \(H \propto 1/f\), so halving f doubles roughness: \(2R_{a1}\).

Quick check

1. In the ideal RC model, MRR is proportional to…

  1. Capacitance C directly
  2. 1 / Rc
  3. Square-root of C
  4. Rc squared directly

C cancels between energy per spark (∝ C) and frequency (∝ 1/C). Only Rc remains: MRR ∝ 1/Rc.

2. What happens if Rc falls below the critical resistance?

  1. Sparks become too weak
  2. The dielectric stops ionising
  3. A continuous arc forms
  4. The servo retracts permanently

The capacitor cannot relax; current flows continuously and the gap never deionises. Arcing damages tool and work.

3. Which change improves EDM surface finish?

  1. Lower capacitance per spark
  2. Higher breakdown voltage setting
  3. Lower sparking frequency setting
  4. Higher peak current setting

Smaller C means less energy per spark, smaller craters and lower Ra. The other three all make craters bigger or fewer.

4. Crater depth h in the theory scales as…

  1. C Vb
  2. C½ Vb
  3. C Vb2
  4. C1/3 Vb2/3

Crater volume ∝ h³ ∝ energy ½CVb², so h ∝ (CVb²)1/3.
